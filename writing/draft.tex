\chapter*{Abstract}

Computer simulations provide an important technique to investigate biophysical processes that remain difficult to investigate experimentally. All-atom force fields capture every detail, whereas coarse-grained models reduce the degrees of freedom, facilitating the investigation of significantly larger timescales and systems. This work capitalizes on these advantages by utilizing a coarse-grained dissipative particle dynamics (DPD) method based on the MARTINI force field, proposed by Wang and Hernandez (\textit{ACS Omega, 2024}), to investigate membrane self-assembly and the reproducibility of membrane properties. Both serve as a benchmark for this method. DPD serves as a suitable candidate, as it offers faster dynamics, due to soft interactions. The evaluation of self-assembly assesses packing density, lipid alignment, and phase separation of the membrane. A power spectrum analysis and a tensile force measurement determine the bending modulus. Successful self-assembly processes yield structures that closely resemble MARTINI bilayers. However, the increased effective size of DPD beads results in an elevated area per lipid of approximately $0.85\si{\nano\m\squared}$ compared to the MARTINI value of $0.65\si{\nano\m\squared}$. Additionally, the soft DPD interaction reduces the bilayer thickness to $2.9\si{\nano\m}$. This is also reflected in the bending modulus via power spectrum analysis, which is reduced to $5.2k_\text{B}T$. While tensile force measurement confirms the general scale of the bending modulus, this method still poses substantial convergence challenges. The elevated mobility of DPD beads allows for high efficiency; however, the soft-core potential and increased effective size significantly impact the mechanical rigidity and area per lipid of the membrane.

\chapter{Introduction}

Investigating biophysical processes for numerous purposes, such as drug discovery \cite{biophysics_drug_disc} and gaining a deeper understanding of illnesses \cite{biophysics_cancer}, remains a vital task in physics. While experimental techniques are instrumental, some phenomena are challenging to observe. For instance, the finite resolution of microscopes and uncontrollable environmental factors can limit the scope of experiments. In these cases, computer simulation can provide an alternative \cite{all_atom_drug_disc}. The all-atom approach models every single atom in a molecule, including hydrogen, and simulates interactions with great detail. This high accuracy comes at the cost of a rough energy surface and a massive number of degrees of freedom, restricting timescale and system size. To circumvent this problem, coarse-grained simulations map multiple heavy atoms to a single interaction site, reducing the degrees of freedom and introducing a simpler interaction potential, smoothing the energy surface. One popular coarse-graining scheme is the MARTINI model. This transferable method, utilizes the Lennard-Jones potential for non-bonded interactions. While this potential is effective, the steep repulsive walls limit the integration timestep and overall speed of the dynamics. A possible alternative is Dissipative Particle Dynamics (DPD), as proposed by Groot and Warren. It employs a soft-sphere simulation framework governed by three forces: the conservative force $\mathbf F^C$, the dissipative force $\mathbf F^D$, and the random force $\mathbf F^R$. Furthermore, the fluctuation-dissipation theorem connects the dissipative and random forces, enabling simulations within the canonical ensemble. These soft-core interactions and the integrated thermostat offer substantial advantages over alternative force fields.

This work employs a coarse-grained DPD model, using the MARTINI force field as the basis for parametrization \cite{Wang2024}. This approach aims to combine the benefits of coarse-graining with the efficiency of the DPD force field. The study evaluates the DPD model via lipid membrane self-assembly across various system sizes and temperatures, and the membrane properties: area per lipid, membrane thickness, and bending modulus. The investigation focuses on homogeneous membranes composed of 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC).

\cref{ch:theory} introduces coarse-grained simulations as a technique to overcome the challenges of all-atom simulations and defines the MARTINI coarse-graining mechanism. \cref{dpd} examines the DPD force field, including a comparison with the Lennard-Jones potential and a description of the integrated thermostat. Subsequent sections define the remaining interactions, molecular simulation fundamentals, and theoretical background for computations of the bending modulus. \cref{ch:methods} details the transition from MARTINI to coarse-grained DPD, providing a comprehensive description of the lipid system topology and the conversion of key force field parameters into DPD units. Furthermore, this chapter provides the simulation protocols for self-assembly and bending moduli measurements. \cref{ch:results} discusses the obtained results, emphasizing the comparison between DPD and MARTINI, before concluding this thesis in \cref{ch.conclusion}.

\section{Coarse-Grained Simulations}\label{sec:coarse-grained-simulations}

Coarse-grained molecular simulations \cite{CGwater_review} aim to simulate the behavior of large systems and timescales, while preserving the main thermodynamic and
structural properties. The primary technique is a mapping mechanism that
represents groups of atoms as a single interaction site or “bead”. The coarse-graining degree, defining the number of heavy atoms in one bead, vary between different methods and the length scales of interest.
Coarse-graining models provide four major advantages that improve performance \cite{cg_review_speedups}. The mapping mechanism reduces the degrees of freedom, thereby decreasing the number of interactions to compute. Furthermore, omitting atomistic detail smooths the potential energy surface and lowers friction, leading to faster dynamics. Some coarse-grained models employ a truncated interaction force, additionally reducing the amount of computation. While all-atom simulations utilize a timestep on the order of femtoseconds\cite{Atomic_timescales}, coarse-graining allows it to be extended into tens or hundreds of femtoseconds, depending on the model.  
A central challenge in CG simulations is parametrization of the interaction between beads. Two main 
strategies exist: utilizing experimental data (top-down)
or the results from more accurate all-atom simulations (bottom-up) to define the CG interactions.
Since not every thermodynamic property can be captured simultaneously, every coarse-graining strategy involves some tradeoffs \cite{CGwater_review}.
This work employs the mapping mechanism of the MARTINI force field \cite{Martini}, the details of which are explained in 
\cref{martini}. 


\section{The MARTINI Model}\label{martini}

The MARTINI force field \cite{Martini} is one of the most widely used models for coarse-grained simulations. 
The employed mapping mechanism distinguishes between four basic bead 
types: polar (P), non-polar (N), apolar (C), and charged (Q).
The P and C beads are assigned an additional number from 1 to 5 depending on the 
polarity, specifically P5 is the most
polar bead whereas C1 is the most apolar bead.
The water bead is identical to the P4 bead. The Q and N beads also have 
subtypes, quantifying the hydrogen bond forming capabilities. It differentiates between, \textit{d} donors, \textit{a} acceptors, \textit{da} beads acting as both, and \textit{0} beads with no bonding capabilities.
% There is \textit{0} meaning no capabilities, \textit{d} meaning donor capabilities, 
% \textit{a} meaning acceptor capabilities, and \textit{da} meaning both donor and acceptor capabilities.
This finite set of beads is utilized to construct molecules. Crucially, the physical properties and interaction parameters remain consistent for every bead, regardless of the molecular structure to which it belongs.
For instance, an apolar C1 bead within a lipid chain interacts with its environment identically to a C1 bead found in an amino acid. The coarse-graining degree \cite{Martini} is 4:1 for most structures, while smaller structures, like rings, utilize 3:1 or 2:1 mappings.    

The MARTINI model employs the Lennard-Jones potential to describe the interaction for a non-bonded bead pair ($i,j$)

\begin{equation}
U^{LJ}(\epsilon_{ij}, \sigma_{ij}) = 4 \epsilon_{ij} \left [\left(\frac{\sigma_{ij}}{r} \right)^{12}
- \left(\frac{\sigma_{ij}}{r} \right)^6 \right ]\, . \label{LJ_pot}
\end{equation}

In this expression, $\epsilon_{ij}$ represents the well depth,  dictating the strength of the attraction between the beads according to an underlying interaction matrix. The parameter $\sigma_{ij}$ denotes the distance at which the potential equals zero, effectively defining the size of the beads.

\section{Dissipative Particle Dynamics} \label{dpd}
The DPD force field was introduced by Groot and Warren for the simulation of systems at 
the mesoscopic scale \cite{DPD_groot_warren} and is widely used for fluid simulations.
The equations of motion for a particle $i$ interacting with surrounding particles $j$
in a DPD force field are governed by

\begin{equation}
    m \frac{\text{d}\mathbf{v}_i}{\text{d}t} = \sum_{j \neq i} \mathbf{F}^C_{ij} + 
    \mathbf{F}^D_{ij} + \mathbf{F}^R_{ij}\, ,  
\end{equation}

consisting of three different components: the conservative
force $\mathbf{F}^C$, the dissipative force $\mathbf{F}^D$ and the random force $\mathbf{F}^R$. 
The conservative force is defined as

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{F}^C_{ij} = \begin{cases}
        A_{ij}\left (1 - \frac{r_{ij}}{r_c} \right )\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}, & r_{ij} \leq r_c \\
        0, & r_{ij} > r_c 
    \end{cases}
    \, , \label{eq:dpd_conservative}
\end{equation}

where $r_c$ represents the cutoff distance and $A_{ij}$ is the maximum repulsion between 
a specific particle pair. Furthermore, the relative position by
$\mathbf{r}_{ij} = \mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j$, with $r_{ij} = |\mathbf{r}_{ij}|$ as its magnitude and $\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij} = \mathbf{r}_{ij}/r_{ij}$ the corresponding unit vector.
Typically, the cutoff distance is set to $r_c = 1$ when working with reduced units, 
simplifying the expression above. \cref{comp_forces} illustrates the difference between the conservative DPD force in \cref{eq:dpd_conservative} and the Lennard-Jones potential in \cref{LJ_pot}.
The key distinction between the two
approaches is that the DPD force and the corresponding potential
do not diverge at $r=0$, allowing for particle overlap and characterizing it as a soft potential. Additionally, DPD is a purely repulsive 
force, whereas Lennard-Jones includes an attractive component and a potential minimum.
Furthermore, the DPD force is a short-range interaction, while the Lennard-Jones force  theoretically has an infinite range but is typically truncated to improve computational efficiency.

The dissipative and random force can best be described by using two weight 
functions $w^D$ and $w^R$. Espanol and Warren examined the statistic of DPD and found the fluctuation dissipation theorem, dictating that the weight functions need to satisfy $w^D(r_{ij}) = \left [w^R(r_{ij}) \right ]^2$. Consequently, defining one weight function fixes the other.
The common choice for the weighting function is

\begin{equation}
    w^D(r_{ij}) = \left [w^R(r_{ij}) \right ]^2 = \begin{cases}
        \left (1 - \frac{r_{ij}}{r_c} \right )^2, & r_{ij} \leq r_c \\
        0, & r_{ij} > r_c
    \end{cases}\, .
\end{equation}

The magnitude of both forces are governed by scaling coefficients: $\sigma$ for the random force
and $\gamma$ for the dissipative force. The fluctuation dissipation theorem connects these
two parameters with the thermal Energy $k_B T$ \cite{DPD_groot_warren} and yields

\begin{equation}
    \sigma^2 = 2 \gamma k_B T\, . \label{connection_fluc_dis}
\end{equation}

By defining $\mathbf{v}_{ij} = \mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{v}_j$ as the
relative velocity between two particles
and $\alpha_{ij}$ as 
a Gaussian noise term with zero mean, unit variance, and $\alpha_{ij} = \alpha_{ji}$, 
the final expression for both forces \cite{DPD_groot_warren} is obtained

\begin{align}
    \mathbf{F}^D_{ij} &= -\gamma w^D(r_{ij})(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}\cdot \mathbf{v}_{ij})
    \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}\, , \\
    \mathbf{F}^R_{ij} &= \sigma w^R(r_{ij}) \alpha_{ij} \Delta t^{-1/2} \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}\, .
\end{align}

Notably, a factor of $\Delta t^{-1/2}$ appears in the random force expression. This correction
follows the work of Groot and Warren \cite{DPD_groot_warren} and
ensures that the mean square displacement satisfies the correct time dependency
$\left < x^2\right >\propto t$.
As previously mentioned, both forces are linked by the fluctuation dissipation theorem 
\cref{connection_fluc_dis}. This assures that the energy added by the random force
$\mathbf{F}^R$ 
is exactly balanced by the energy dissipated through force $\mathbf{F}^D$. Consequently, both forces act as an internal 
thermostat, allowing sampling of the canonical ensemble and eliminating the need for any
external thermostats during simulations.

To entirely parametrize DPD interactions, only the 
repulsion parameters $A_{ij}$ and the drag coefficient $\gamma$ need to be determined, fixing the 
random force parameter $\sigma$. This step will be discussed further in \cref{sec:dpd_parameters}.

\section{Electrostatic Interactions}\label{coulomb}

The DPD force field in \cref{dpd} does not differentiate between a charged or an uncharged bead. Therefore, 
an electrostatic interaction must be integrated into the force field, 
or the repulsions have to be adjusted accordingly. Crucially, a simple 
Coulomb potential is unsuitable, as its divergence at $r=0$ would negate the softness of the DPD potential. To address this, 
an exponentially smeared charge distribution, resulting in a Slater potential, is employed 
. This follows the work of Wang and Hernandez \cite{Wang2024}
who introduce a factor $\beta$ which controls the extend of the
smearing effect. The charge distribution 

\begin{equation}
    \rho(r) = \frac{q\beta^2}{\pi r} \exp(-2\beta r) \label{charge_density}
\end{equation}

utilizies the constant $\Gamma = e^2/(k_\text{B}T\epsilon_0\epsilon_rr_c)$ with $\epsilon_r = 78.3$. The resulting electrostatic force between two beads $i$ and $j$ with valences $Z_i$ and $Z_j$ follows

\begin{align}
    \mathbf{F}^E_{ij} = \frac{\Gamma Z_i Z_j}{4 \pi r_{ij}^2}[1 - \exp(-2\beta r_{ij})
    (1+2\beta r_{ij}(1+\beta r_{ij}))]\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}\, . \label{coulomb_force}
\end{align}


\section{Intramolecular Interactions}\label{intermol}

Beyond the non-bonded interactions defined in \cref{dpd} and \cref{coulomb}, intramolecular potentials
involving various numbers of participating beads ensure the structural integrity of the molecules \cite{Martini}.
Two-body interactions are defined as bonds and determine the equilibrium distance
between two beads,
while angle potentials control the local geometry between three consecutive beads. 
Four-body interactions utilize
a proper dihedral potential to characterize the torsion around a central bond, preserving 
properties such as the orientation of specific atomic groups. Lastly, improper dihedrals 
maintain the chirality of molecular groups.

The specific potentials depend on the molecule being represented, and not every molecule utilizes every
type of interaction. A more detailed application of these potentials will be discussed in \cref{topology}.

\section{Numerical Integration}

As no analytical solutions exist for the Newtonian equations of motion in molecular systems, numerical integration is required. These algorithms employ stepwise computation to resolve the trajectories of each particle by propagating positions and velocities over discrete timesteps $\Delta t$. This process enables the time evolution of the system, where time averaging equals the corresponding ensemble average, provided the applied integrator conserves the matching properties.

The forces defined in \cref{dpd}, \cref{coulomb}, and \cref{intermol} result in a system of ordinary differential equations that molecular dynamics simulations must solve. The Velocity Verlet Integrator \cite{Allen2017_verlet} serves as a primary algorithm for this task, computing the position and velocity of a bead at $t+\Delta t$ based on the state at the previous timestep $t$. The iterative procedure follows:

\begin{alignat}{2}
&1.\ \text{Predict half-step velocity} \quad &&\mathbf{v}(t + \Delta t/2) = \mathbf{v}(t) +
\mathbf{a}(t) \Delta t/2\\
&2.\ \text{Update positions} \quad&&\mathbf{x}(t + \Delta t) = \mathbf{x}(t) +
\mathbf{v}(t + \Delta t/2)\Delta t\\
&3.\ \text{Calculate new acceleration} \quad&&\mathbf{a}(t + \Delta t) =
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}(t + \Delta t), \mathbf{v}(t + \Delta t/2))/m\\
&4.\ \text{Correct final velocity} \quad&&\mathbf{v}(t + \Delta t) =
\mathbf{v}(t + \Delta t/2) + \mathbf{a}(t + \Delta t)\Delta t/2.
\end{alignat}

This scheme is particularly suitable for DPD, as the dissipative force depends on velocity. Utilizing the intermediate velocity at $t+\Delta t/2$ allows for a more accurate treatment of these forces. Velocity Verlet functions as a symplectic integrator, which ensures energy conservation over large timespans and facilitates sampling of the microcanonical ($NVE$) ensemble. The addition of the DPD force field conserves the temperature, enabling simulation of the canonical ($NVT$) ensemble.


\section{Periodic Boundary Conditions}

\begin{figure}[t]

    \label{fig:pbc}

Computer simulation face the challenge of being limited to finite system sizes. This 
limitation leads to unphysical surface effects when particles reach the boundaries of the system. 
The standard mechanism is to enforce a periodic boundary condition (PBC) \cite{LammpsBoundary}. Employing a 
PBC means that a particle leaving the simulation box simultaneously re-enters from the opposing boundary. Furthermore, 
a particle near a boundary also interacts with the particles on the other side of the box via the nearest
image convention, meaning that any particle only interacts with the closest periodic image of its neighbors. 
Effectively, this creates
a system that behaves like an infinite grid of the same finite system. The effect of the PBC is 
visualized in \cref{fig:pbc}.

\section{Assembly Metrics}\label{nematic}

The degree of order in a system is a critical metric when simulating self-assembly, as 
it quantifies the macroscopic state of the system. The nematic order parameter 
\cite{Ramasubramani2020_freud}, \cite{FreudNematicDoc} measures 
the collective alignment of particles. The parameter is derived from the traceless second-order
tensor $\mathbf{Q}$, defined as 

\begin{align}
    \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{M} - \frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\, ,
\end{align}

where $\mathbf{M}$ is the orientation tensor and $\mathbf{I}$ the identity matrix.
The components of $\mathbf{M}$ are calculated using the normalized orientation vector $\mathbf{m}^{(i)}$ 
of the molecule $i \in \{1,\dots,N \}$. For a supposed molecule with a head bead $\mathbf{r}_h$ and tail $\mathbf{r}_t$ bead this yields

\begin{align}
    \mathbf{m} = \frac{\mathbf{r}_h - \mathbf{r}_t}{|\mathbf{r}_h - \mathbf{r}_t|}\, .
\end{align}

The orientation matrix follows directly as

\begin{align}
    \mathbf{M}_{\alpha \beta} = \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{m}_\alpha^{(i)}\mathbf{m}_\beta^{(i)}\, .
\end{align}

The eigenvalues of $\mathbf{Q}$ are $\frac{2}{3}S$, $-\frac{1}{3}S$ and $-\frac{1}{3}S$, where $S$ represents
the desired order parameter. The eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue is called the 
director, indicating the principal alignment of the system. 

In addition to alignment, the packing density is quantified to ensure that the lipids form a connected structure. For this purpose, a 
contact number is defined as the average number of neighboring lipids within a specific cutoff distance $r_c$. To this end the distance matrix $\mathbf{\Delta}_{ij} = |\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j|$ is defined, where $\mathbf{r}_i$ is the center of mass of molecule $i$. Using the criterion 

\begin{align}
    f(r, r_c) = \begin{cases}
        1, & if r \leq r_c \\
        0, & else 
    \end{cases}
\end{align}

the contact number $\langle n \rangle$ can be expressed as 

\begin{align}
    \langle n \rangle = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i = 1}^N \sum_{j \neq i} f(\mathbf{\Delta}_{ij}, r_c)\, .
\end{align}


This metric is highly dependent on this cutoff parameter, which is determined in \cref{sec:128_assembly}.

\section{Membrane Fluctuations} \label{sec:ben_mod_fourier}

The bending modulus details how much force is needed to deform a membrane. Thus, it is instructive to investigate the fluctuations of a bilayer.
The deformation energy of a membrane \cite{Markus_tethers} is defined as an integral over the 
membrane area, governed by two moduli: the bending modulus $\kappa$ and
the saddle splay modulus $\bar{\kappa}$. It reads

\begin{align}
    E = \int \mathrm d A \left[ \frac{1}{2}\kappa 
    (K - C_0)^2 + \bar{\kappa}K_G \right]\, , \label{eq:helfrich_energy}
\end{align}

where $K$ is the mean curvature, $K_G$ the Gaussian curvature and $C_0$ the intrinsic curvature.
Utilizing the Monge parametrization with a height function $h(x,y)$, the mean curvature \cite{Markus_tethers}
of an almost flat membrane can be approximated as

\begin{align}
    K = \nabla \cdot \left( \frac{\nabla h}{\sqrt{1 + (\nabla h)^2}}\right)
    \approx \Delta h \, .
\end{align}

Including the lateral tension $\Sigma$, this results in the following energy expression \cite{Cooke_bending_mod}

\begin{align}
    E = \frac{1}{2} \int \mathrm d\mathbf x \left[ 
        \kappa (\Delta h(\mathbf x))^2 + \Sigma (\nabla h(\mathbf x))^2 \right]\, . \label{eq:mem_energy}
\end{align}

To further investigate
the membrane fluctuations \cite{Schwarz2024},
the height function is expressed 
via its inverse Fourier transform 

\begin{align}
    h(\mathbf x) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)} \int \mathrm d\mathbf k 
    \tilde{h}(\mathbf k) \exp(i\mathbf k \cdot \mathbf x)\, , 
\end{align}

with the wave vector $\mathbf k$. It is instructive to introduce 
the dirac delta distribution represented in Fourier space 

\begin{align}
    (2\pi)^2 \delta(\mathbf k) = \int \mathrm d \mathbf x \exp(i\mathbf k \cdot \mathbf x)\, .
\end{align}

The height function is purely real. Consequently, $h(\mathbf x) = h(\mathbf x)^*$ leads to 
the relation in Fourier space $h(\mathbf k)^* = h(-\mathbf k)$. Inserting the results into 
\cref{eq:mem_energy} yields 

\begin{align}
    E = \frac{1}{2} \int \mathrm d \mathbf k 
    |\tilde{h}(\mathbf k)|^2 \left[\kappa k^4 + \Sigma k^2 \right]\, .
\end{align}

Applying the equipartition theorem, which relates the average of each squared mode  $\langle |\tilde{h}
(\mathbf k)|^2\rangle$ to the thermal energy of $k_{\mathrm B} T/2 $, leads to the 
expression 

\begin{align}
    \langle |\tilde{h}(\mathbf k)|^2\rangle = \frac{k_{\mathrm B}T}{\kappa k^4 + \Sigma k^2}\, .
\end{align}

For practical application, a discrete Fourier transform 

\begin{align}
    h(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)}\sum_\mathbf{k} \tilde{h}_\mathbf{k} \exp(i \mathbf k \cdot \mathbf x) 
\end{align}

is employed
with discrete wave vectors $\mathbf k = 2\pi/L\cdot (n_x,n_y)$.
For a membrane of the area $A = L^2$, this discrete approach leads to the final expression for the fluctuation power spectrum

\begin{align}
    \langle |\tilde{h}_\mathbf{k}|^2\rangle = \frac{k_{\mathrm B}T}{L^2(\kappa k^4 + \Sigma k^2)}\, . \label{eq:cental_ben_formula_fourier}
\end{align}

Notably, an additional factor of $1/L^2$ arises from the transition to a discrete basis. In this context, the continuous Dirac delta distribution $\delta(\mathbf{k} + \mathbf{k})$ is replaced by its discrete counterpart $L^2 \delta_{\mathbf{k}, -\mathbf{k}}$. Dimensionality analysis confirms this substitution, as $[\delta(\mathbf{k})]$ and $[L^2 \delta_{\mathbf{k}, -\mathbf{k}}]$ possess the dimensions of area. 

\section{Stretching Tethers}\label{sec:methods_tethers}

Active induction of curvature through cylindrical membranes provides an alternative to membrane fluctuation measurements for computing the bending modulus. Applying the energy expression from \cref{eq:helfrich_energy} to a cylindrical membrane geometry follows the approach of Harmandaris and Deserno \cite{Markus_tethers}. A cylinder with radius $R$ possesses a mean curvature of $K=1/R$.
Harmandaris and Deserno \cite{Markus_tethers} suggest that the contribution from the Gaussian curvature and saddle splay modulus $\bar{\kappa}$ remains constant, allowing its omission from this analysis. Integration over the surface area yields the curvature energy for a cylinder of length $L_z$ aligned along the z-axis

\begin{align}
E = \frac{\kappa}{2} \left(\frac{1}{R} \right)^2 2\pi RL_z
= \frac{\pi \kappa L_z}{R}\, .
\end{align}

Maintaining a cylindrical shape requires the application of a force $F_z$. Taking the derivative of the energy with respect to $L_z$ under fixed surface area constraints links this force directly to the bending modulus

\begin{align}
F_z = \frac{\partial E}{\partial L_z} = \frac{2 \pi \kappa}{R}\, .
\end{align}

The relationship $F_z = \sigma_{zz}L_x L_y$ connects the force $F_z$ to the stress tensor. The final expression for the bending modulus reads

\begin{align}
\kappa = \frac{F_z R}{2\pi}\, .\label{eq:tether_central_from}
\end{align}

\section{System Topology}\label{topology}

The relevant system in this work is a mixture of POPC lipids and water as solvent. 
The molecular topology from the MARTINI 2 model is defined in the \path{martini_v_2.0_POPC.itp} file, 
obtained from the MARTINI website \cite{Martini,MartiniLipidome}. It serves as the basis for the 
parametrization in the following sections. It lists the employed potential for the 
bonds and angles, including their equilibrium parameters in MARTINI units, as well
as the bead types used in the representation.

\cref{martini} introduced the basic bead types in the MARTINI method. In this work the system consists 
of POPC lipids and water molecules acting as the solvent. Each water molecule is modeled as a P4 bead and 
contains four classical water molecules. 
The POPC lipids consist of a choline head group (Q0, bead 1), a phosphate group (Qa, bead 2) and two 
glycerol groups (Na, beads 3 and 4) that connect the polar head to two hydrophobic tails. These tails are comprised of an oleoyl (C1, beads 5, 6, 8; C3, bead 7)
and a palmitoyl group (C1, beads 9, 10, 11, 12) and are highly apolar. Each bead is assigned a unique ID ranging from
one to twelve to precisely reference specific bead. The resulting coarse-grained molecule, consisting of 
five unique bead types and twelve total beads
is illustrated in \cref{fig:popc_lammps}. The relative bead positions are based on MARTINI mapping and were extracted from the 
\path{POPC-em.gro} file \cite{Martini,MartiniLipidome}. The beads are connected by eleven bonds and eight angles, categorized into 
two unique bond types and three angle types, as detailed in \cref{tab:bond_angle_connections}.
There are no dihedral interactions in the POPC molecule.

While \cref{intermol} defined the general interaction between beads inside a molecule, the specific bond potential is implemented as a simple harmonic potential
in LAMMPS \cite{LammpsBond} 

\begin{equation}
    E_{ij} = \hat{K}^b(r_{ij} - r^0_{ij})^2. \label{harmonic}\, ,
\end{equation}

where $K_b$ represents the bond stiffness, containing the usual 
factor of $1/2$, and $r^0_{ij}$ is the equilibrium
bond distance of the bead pair.
Similarly, the angle potential \cite{LammpsAngle} is a cosine-squared function

\begin{equation}
    E_{ijk} = \hat{K}^a[\cos(\theta_{ijk}) - \cos(\theta^0_{ijk})]^2\, , \label{cosine/squared}
\end{equation}


where $K_a$ serves as the angular stiffness and  $\theta^0_{ijk}$ denotes the equilibrium angle between the three beads. Although this choice 
is dictated by the MARTINI force field, it is worth noting why a cosine based potential is preferred over a simple harmonic one. The formulation in \cref{cosine/squared}
is significantly more computationally efficient, as it avoids the use of the $\arccos(\theta)$ function, which is expensive and numerically sensitive.
A detailed computation of the equilibrium parameters is done in \cref{sec:dpd_bond_params}.

\section{DPD Units}\label{dpd_units}

Molecular dynamics simulations frequently employ reduced units,
which utilize reference parameters to render physical quantities
dimensionless. For MARTINI these are typically the Lennard-Jones parameters 
$\epsilon$ and $\sigma$, defined in \cref{LJ_pot}. However, since DPD utilizes a different 
force and potential, an alternative set of reference parameters is required. Following the work of Wang and Hernandez \cite{Wang2024}, the length scale $r_\text{ref}$ is defined based on the volume required to contain three CG water beads. This 
choice is not arbitrary as it 
follows the early
work of Groot and Warren 
\cite{DPD_groot_warren} who established a standard number density of $\rho=3$ DPD beads per volume $V = r_{\text{ref}}^3 $. 
In the MARTINI model, every water bead represents $N_w = 4$ classical molecules. 
The volume of a single water molecule, $V_w$, can be calculated using its
molar mass $M=18.02 \si{\g\per\mol}$,
the density $\rho_w = 1 \si{\g\per\cubic\centi\meter}$ and the Avogadro
constant $N_A = 6.022 \cdot 10^{23} 
\text{molecules}/\text{mol}$

\begin{equation}
    V_w = \frac{M}{\rho_w N_A} = 2.99 \cdot 10^{-23} \si{\cubic\centi\m} 
    = 29.9 \si{\cubic\angstrom}\, .
\end{equation}

The DPD reference length follows directly from the previous computations

\begin{equation}
    r_{\text{ref}} = \sqrt[3]{\rho \cdot N_w \cdot V_w} = 7.11 \si{\angstrom}\, ,
\end{equation}

which serves as the basis to convert any physical length into DPD units through normalization by $r_\text{ref}$.

The energy scale is defined more directly. By choosing the reference temperature as
$T_{\text{ref}}=298.15 \si{\K}$, the thermal energy at this temperature is used as reference unit 

\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_{\text{ref}} = k_{\mathrm{B}} T_{\text{ref}} = 4.12 \cdot 10^{-21} \si{\J} = 
    25.7 \si{\milli\eV}\, .
\end{equation}

Similar to the length scale, this energy scale allows for the conversion of every energy value to 
be converted into DPD units by division. Furthermore, the combination of these two scales can be 
employed to render physical quantities dimensionless. 

Since this work aims to investigate dynamic processes, specifically the self-assembly, it is crucial
to establish a timescale. While this usually entails a simple combination of the 
existing scales, the DPD timescale has to be handled with more care. 
Following the work of Wang and Hernandez \cite{Wang2024} the timescale is calculated by 
matching the diffusion constant of DPD water to its experimental value. This leads to 
a timescale of 

\begin{align}
    \tau = 91.4\si{\pico\s}\, ,
\end{align}

which serves as a conversion from DPD time to real time. For instance, $1000$ steps with a timestep of $\Delta \hat{t} = 0.01$ leads to a total DPD time of $10\tau$ equal to $10 \cdot 91.4\si{\pico\s} = 0.914 \si{\nano\s}$ in real time.


\section{Determination of DPD Bonded Parameters}\label{sec:dpd_bond_params}

The MARTINI 2 model provides the equilibrium parameters, as detailed in \cref{topology}. The MARTINI database \cite{Martini,MartiniLipidome} specifies these parameters in SI units, specifically, it uses 
$[r] = \si{\nano\m}$ for distances and $[k^b] = \si{\kilo\J\per\mol\per\nano\m\squared}$ for the bond stiffness.
Similarly, the database defines the angle stiffness in $[k^a] = \si{\kilo\J\per\mol}$.

The conversion into DPD units employs the reference scales defined in \cref{dpd_units}.
Besides the application of the scales, the MARTINI units are divided by the Avogadro constant to account for their molar basis.

constant in DPD unit, $\hat{K}^b$, follows as 

\begin{align}

\end{align}

The conversion of the equilibrium distances requires a simple scaling

\begin{align}
    \hat{r}^0 = r^0/r_{\text{ref}}\, .
\end{align}

\begin{align}

\section{Determination of DPD Charges}

Following the introduction in \cref{coulomb}, a slater potential models the coulombic interaction, as detailed in \cref{charge_density} and \cref{coulomb_force}. The model utilizes a strength parameter 
derived from earlier works from Gonzales-Melchor et al. \cite{Gonzales-Melchor_beta}, who 
determine a value of $\beta_3 = 0.929$ for a coarse-graining degree of $N = 3$.
Wang and Hernandez \cite{Wang2024} scaled this factor $\beta$ 
to the applied coarse-graining of $N = 4$ under the assumption of linearity. 
Because the LAMMPS implementation \cite{LammpsSlater} requires the inverse value $\lambda = 1/\beta$, the conversion yields  

    
\begin{align}
    \beta = 0.844 = 1/\lambda = 1/1.185\, .
\end{align}
    
The introduced length and energy scales also impact the charge scaling. To calibrate 
the charge, the potential energy of two point charges in SI units is equated
with the corresponding energy in DPD units \cite{Wang2024,Allen2017_verlet}, 
resulting in

\begin{align}
    &\frac{q^2}{4\pi \epsilon_0 r}  = 
    \frac{\hat{q}^2}{\hat{r}} e_{\text{ref}}\\
    &\hat{q} = \frac{q}{\sqrt{4\pi \epsilon_0 r_{\text{ref}}e_{\text{ref}}}} = 8.86\, .
\end{align}

Under this definition, one elemental charge in SI units maps 
to a dimensionless DPD charge of $\hat{q} = 8.86$. Standard LAMMPS convention absorbs the factors of $4\pi\epsilon_0$ directly into the charge definition.

\section{Determination of DPD Force Parameters}\label{sec:dpd_parameters}

The repulsion parameters $A_{ij}$ constitute the core of the DPD force field,
as they determine the maximum repulsion of the conservative force introduced in \cref{dpd}.
In addition, the model requires the definition of the drag parameter $\gamma$.
The work of Groot and Warren \cite{DPD_groot_warren} provides the basis for this value, identifying $\hat{\sigma} = 3$ \enquote{as a compromise between fast temperature equilibration and a stable, physically meaningful system}.
Applying the fluctuation dissipation theorem in \cref{connection_fluc_dis} and 
setting the thermal energy $k_{\text{B}}T$ to unity yields
 
\begin{align}
    \hat{\gamma} = 4.5\, .
\end{align}

Within LAMMPS, the drag parameter $\hat{\gamma}$ serves as a fixed value in the force field implementation. Changing the target temperature of the DPD force automatically scales
the random force parameter $\hat{\sigma}$, according to the fluctuation dissipation theorem.

Wang and Hernandez \cite{Wang2024} derived the repulsion parameters used in this simulation. 
\cref{tab:dpd_params} presents these values, while the following section details the derivation method.

The repulsion between two water beads $A_{ww}$ serves as the baseline. 
Determining this value involves matching the inverse compressibility $\kappa^{-1}$ from experimental data 
to DPD simulation data. This approach yields 

\begin{align}
    A_{ww} = 102.067\, .
\end{align}


at a number density of $\rho = 3$.

The MARTINI model applies the effective Lennard-Jones radius
$\sigma_{ww}$ from water-water interaction to all identical bead pairs, enforcing the condition 
$\sigma_{AA} = \sigma_{ww}$. Analogously, Wang and Hernandez \cite{Wang2024} assign the determined repulsion parameter $A_{ww}$to every interaction between identical beads, resulting in $A_{AA} = A_{ww}$ for all bead types A.

The following expression defines the interaction between two distinc beads $(A \neq B)$ as

\begin{equation}
    A_{AB} = A_{ww} + \Delta A_{AB}\, .
\end{equation}

The parameter $\Delta A_{AB}$ is related to the Flory-Huggins parameter 

\begin{equation}
    \chi_{AB} = \lambda_\chi \Delta A_{AB}\, 
\end{equation}

where the fitting parameter $\lambda_\chi$ links the two models.
Earlier work by Groot and Rabone \cite{GROOT_xi_scale} demonstrate that the Flory-Huggins 
parameter $\chi_{AB}$ scales linearly with the coarse-graining degree, $N_m = 4$
molecules per bead, yielding 

\begin{equation}
    \chi_{AB} = \bar{\chi}_{AB} N_m\, .
\end{equation}

The final expression for the repulsion parameter reads

\begin{equation}
    A_{AB} = A_{ww} + \bar{\chi}_{AB} N_m/\lambda_\chi\, . \label{final_form_dpd}
\end{equation}

Determining the fitting parameter $\lambda_\chi$ requires a phase seperation simulation within a box initially containing two different bead types $A$ and $B$.
The simulation measures the equilibrium volume fraction $\phi$. Repeating this procedure for 
different values of $\Delta a_{AB}$ provides the necessary data point for the calculation. For each run, the measured volume fraction determines the Flory-Huggins parameter $\chi$ according to

\begin{equation}
    \chi = \frac{\ln[(1-\phi)/\phi]}{1 - 2\phi}\, .
\end{equation}

A linear fit of the resulting data, plotted against the parameter $\Delta A_{AB}$, identifies the fitting parameter from its slope 

\begin{align}
    \lambda_\chi = 0.28\, .
\end{align}

Wang and Hernandez \cite{Wang2024} utilize a baseline interaction matrix 
$\epsilon_{AB}$ to calculate the size-dependant Flory-Hugigns parameter.

\begin{equation}
    \bar{\chi}_{AB}N_m = \chi_{AB} = \frac{z}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T} \left[\epsilon_{AB} - 
    \frac{1}{2} (\epsilon_{AA} + \epsilon_{BB}) \right] \, ,
\end{equation}

where $z$ represents the effective number of nearest neighbors.
The MARTINI model \cite{Martini} serves as the baseline interaction matrix. Substituting the resulting values into \cref{final_form_dpd} yields the final repulsion coefficient. 
This work employs the parameters displayed in \cref{tab:dpd_params} for every simulation.

\section{System Setup}\label{sec:sys_setup}

Initiating a simulation in LAMMPS requires an input files. A header specifies
the number of beads, the count of bond, angle, dihedral, improper types (\cref{intermol}),
the box dimensions and the bead masses. Following the header, specific sections define the position of every beads (atoms sections), the connections by bonds (bonds section), the connections by angles (angles sections). The file concludes with the equilibrium parameters for each bond and angle type.
Although dihedral interactions typically require dedicated sections, this work omits them as previously discussed.

A Python script is used to generate this file using several inputs: the number of lipids $N_l$, the number density $\rho$, the area per lipid $A_l$, and the water-to-lipid ratio $R_{w/l} = M_w/N_l$, where $N_w$ denotes the quantity of water beads. 

\begin{align}
    A_l = \frac{L_x L_y}{N_l/2}\, , \label{eq:def_apl}
\end{align}

defines the area per lipid, assuming a square bilayer in the $xy$-plane with dimensions $L_x$ and $L_y$. The number density 
is a set parameter at $\rho=3$. This not only follows the work of Groot and Warren \cite{DPD_groot_warren} but is also mandatory as Wang and Hernandez \cite{Wang2024} determined the repulsion parameters at $\rho = 3$.
The following sections examine the other two inputs, the area per lipid $A_l$ and the water-to-lipid ratio $R_{w/l}$ in greater detail.

\cref{topology} and \cref{tab:bond_angle_connections} provide the specific counts of for each bead, bond, angle, improper and dihedral type. For the POPC-water system, this configuration yields six bead types, two bond types and three angle types.

The script computes the total quantities of beads, bonds, and angles using the per-molecule definitions from \cref{tab:bond_angle_connections} and the input parameters $N_l$ and $R_{w/l}$

\begin{align}
    &N_\text{beads} = \lfloor(12 + R_{w/l}) \cdot N_l\rfloor \label{eq:num_beads}\\ 
    &N_\text{bonds} = 11 \cdot N_l\\
    &N_\text{angles} = 8 \cdot N_l\, .
\end{align}

The box dimensions derive from the area per lipid, the number density, and the total bead count determined in \cref{eq:num_beads}

\begin{align}
    V &= \frac{N_\text{beads}}{\rho}\\
    L_x = L_y &= \sqrt{N_l/2 \cdot A_l}\\
    L_z &= \frac{V}{L_x L_y}\, .
\end{align}

Assigning a mass of unity to each bead in DPD units, $\hat{m} = 1$, completes the necessary header information.

The bonds and angles section incorporate the connectivity data from \cref{tab:bond_angle_connections} for every POPC molecule. Similarly, \cref{tab:bond_angle_params} provides the equilibrium parameters. 

The remaining atoms section establishes the starting position of each bead. As these coordinates depend on the target configuration, the following sections describe the specific initialization procedures.

\section{Optimization of Input Parameters}\label{sec:input_opt}

Optimization of the remaining input parameters must precede the investigation of the self-assembly process, as utilizing random values of $A_l$ and $R_{w/l}$ would, in general, not allows the formation of a membrane. A large grid search testing every combination of area per lipid $A_l$ and water-to-lipid ratio $R_{w/l}$ for membrane stability, would identify the optimal parameter pairing at the global maximum. However, this approach would be computationally expensive, as it would simulate potentially irrelevant combinations. To bypass this procedure, this work employs an 
initial guess for the water to lipid ratio $R_{w/l}^{\text{init}} \approx 22$. 

Maintaining the initial ratio alongside the fixed number density, 
numerous simulations of a pre-assembled bilayer configuration consisting of 128 lipids are performed, while
varying the area per lipid.

Finally, placing water beads outside the leaflets completes the atoms section and provides the solvent environment. \cref{fig:128_bilayer_setup} illustrates this setup for a system of 128 lipids.

This study comprises 15 simulations performed with 128 POPC lipids and an initial water-to-lipid ratio of $R_{w/l}=22$. The simulations explore area-per-lipid values ranging from $A_l = 0.5\si{\nano\m\squared}$ to $1.2\si{\nano\m\squared}$ at temperatures of $\hat{T}=1.0$ and $0.8$. Subsequent sections investigate the significance of these temperature choices. Each run covers a duration of $3000\tau$ DPD time units with a timestep of $\Delta \hat{t} = 0.01$, following the protocol established by Wang and Hernandez \cite{Wang2024}. To quantify the bilayer quality, the analysis utilizes the 
nematic order parameter introduced in \cref{nematic}. The calculation covers the last $2000 \tau$
of the DPD trajectories to discard effects arising from the initial grid configuration. This procedure employs the head-to-tail vector

\begin{align}

\end{align}

where the subscript is the corresponding bead ID,
as the orientation vector $\vec{m}$ for determining the order parameter. 

The next investigation examines the water-to-lipid ratio to validate the initial guess. While following the previous methodology, the study now varies the water-to-lipid ratio instead of the area per lipid. During these runs, the area per lipid remains fixed at the optimal value identified in the preceding simulations. Establishing this optimal parameter pairing finally allows the self-assembly process to begin.

\section{Spontaneous Self-Assembly Protocol} \label{sec:128_assembly}

To investigate self-assembly, the lipid and water molecules are dispersed randomly throughout the 
box. The script adjusts the atom section to generate a disordered configuration, a lipid soup, instead of the pre-assembled bilayer formation described previously.
The initialization assigns a random center of mass to each water and lipid molecule. To ensure random alignment, the procedure adds individual rotations around every axis to each lipid. Applying the periodic box condition retains every molecule within the designated box. 

This procedure highlights a significant advantage of DPD. While standard MD simulations require great care to prevent bead overlaps, which would result in high-energy starting configurations, the soft nature of the DPD minimizes the penalty for overlapping particles. Consequently, the simulation remains stable even with this simple setup scheme. \cref{fig:lipid_soup_methods} illustrates the corresponding system before self-assembly.

The system is equilibrated by running it at an elevated temperature of $\hat{T} = 2$
where high thermal energy prevents premature self-assembly. To minimize large temperature fluctuations
during the initial phase, every atom is assigned a random velocity following the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution, 
consistent with the starting temperature. Gradually lowering the temperature to $\hat{T} = 1$, induces bilayer formation by modifying the temperature argument of the DPD force.
However, simply updating this value causes the DPD thermostat to adjust the random force parameter $\hat{\sigma} = \sqrt{2\hat{\gamma}\hat{T}}$ instantaneously, which prevents a controlled temperature ramp.
To emulate a continuous cooling process, the simulation utilizes a loop that incrementally updates the target DPD temperature every few steps. 
After reaching the target temperature of $\hat{T} = 1$, a longer production phase
stabilizes the resulting structure.
To evaluate how lower temperatures influence the quality of the final structure, a second set of simulations repeats this procedure using a production
temperature of $\hat{T} = 0.8$. In total the self-assembly protocol simulated $6000\tau$ DPD timeunits with a timestep of $\Delta \hat{t} = 0.01$.

\section{Implementation of Larger Systems}\label{sec:larger_systems}

To evaluate the finite size effect and the ability to generalize to larger systems, a system containing double the amount of lipids 256
is utilized. While the setup mirrors the 128-lipid assembly in \cref{sec:128_assembly}, the change in system size necessitates fine-tuning of the area per lipid and the water-to-lipid ratio. This process involves slight adjustments to both parameters to ensure optimal results.

The simulation initiates at $\hat{T} = 2$ with an 
identical velocity initialization. Ensuring proper dispersion and preventing unwanted clumping of lipid tails necessitates a longer equilibration phase. The cooling 
process then proceeds until the system reaches the target temperature of $\hat{T} = 1$.

This initial configuration yields a bilayer within a non-cubic area where 
the area per lipid remains below the optimal value.
To achieve a square-shaped bilayer, the simulation gradually deforms the box, adjusting the dimensions while maintaining a constant volume.   

After the deformation of the box a production phase follows at $\hat{T} = 1$ to stabilize the structure. Due to the increased system size, a longer total simulation time is required, yielding $9000\tau$ DPD timeunits with a timestep of $\Delta \hat{t} = 0.01$. 

\section{Simulation of Membrane Fluctuations}\label{sec:method_ben_mod_fourier}

The determination of the POPC membrane bending modulus utilizes the theoretical foundation of \cref{sec:ben_mod_fourier}. This process involves measuring the power spectrum of each mode in \cref{eq:cental_ben_formula_fourier} and applying a corresponding fit to derive the bending modulus. However, several physical constraints complicate this procedure


Continuum theory breaks down when the inverse wave vector 1/k becomes comparable to the bilayer thickness w. Consequently, the investigation must focus on small wave vectors. However, for values smaller than the critical wavelength $k_c = \sqrt{\Sigma/\kappa}$ the lateral tension dominates. The simulations must therefore run at zero lateral tension to ensure the target scaling of $k^{-4}$. Maintaining this condition by manually adjusting the box dimensions is difficult \cite{Cooke_bending_mod}. To circumvent this, a Nose/Hoover barostat \cite{LammpsNPH} adjusts the box volume to maintain a specific target pressure, which is simply the average pressure given by LAMMPS for simulations without a barostat. Choosing a low damping parameter, which governs the reaction speed of the barostat, ensures the achievement of zero tension, which requires using a reduced timestep of $\Delta \hat{t} = 0.001$. The timestep adjustment is central as for low damping parameters a large change in pressure leads to a large change in the box volume. If the time step is too large pressure spikes are more common, leading to oscillation or heavy reshaping of the box dimensions. The system must be substantially larger than the self-assembly setup to accommodate small wave vectors.

The simulation is initialized with a pre-assembled membrane containing 4096 lipids, following the methodology of \cref{sec:input_opt} and the work of Cook and Deserno \cite{Cooke_bending_mod}. \cref{fig:fluc_meas_setup} illustrates the setup of the large bilayer. After an equilibration phase of 100$\tau$ to eliminate grid-initialization effects, 100 samples of the system, separated by $10 \tau$, are taken across the $1000\tau$ measurement run. To generate a height function the mean tail position of each lipid is interpolated on a $16 \times 16$ grid. This interpolation requires careful handling, as it introduces discretization artifacts, discussed by Cook and Deserno \cite{Cooke_bending_mod}.
The calculation must account for a damping factor for the two-dimensional wave numbers $(n_x, n_y)$ and an $N \times N$ grid

\begin{align}
    \mathrm{sinc}^2\left(\frac{n_x}{N}\right)\mathrm{sinc}^2\left(\frac{n_y}{N}\right)\, .
\end{align}

Dividing out this factor resolves its $k$-dependence, which would otherwise interfere with the results. The grid data is processed by a two-dimensional Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). The absolute value squared is averaged and is ordered into bins of corresponding $k$ values. As the lateral tension is zero a simple $k^{-4}$ fit of

\begin{align}
    L^2 \langle |\tilde{h}_k|^2 \rangle \approx \frac{k_\text{B}T}{\kappa} k^{-4}
\end{align}

reveals the bending modulus of the POPC membrane. \cref{fig:spectrum_pipeline_final_aligned} visualizes the pipeline of the bending modulus measurement.

\section{Simulation of Tethers} \label{sec:methods_sim_tethers}

Configuring the membrane as a cylinder requires a specific setup, a procedure that mirrors the flat bilayer configuration but introduces additional complexity. The generation script incorporates the number of lipids, the number density, the area per lipid, and the inner radius of the cylinder. The setup fixes the number of lipids at $N_l = 4096$, the number density at $\rho = 3$, and the area per lipid at $0.88\si{{\nano}{\m}{\squared}}$. Different simulation runs utilize varied inner radii. A cylindrical setup necessitates adjustments to the box dimensions and the calculation of starting positions.



Determining the box dimensions and the number of lipids in the outer and inner leaflets is required for proper system initialization. The inner leaflet contains fewer lipids to account for the reduced available area. Adjusting the area per lipid from \cref{eq:def_apl} for a cylindrical geometry aligned along the $z$-axis yields

\begin{align}
    A_l = \frac{2\pi R L_z}{N_l/2}\, .\label{eq:apl_cylinder}
\end{align}



The radius $R = R_\text{in}/2\cdot(1 + d)$ is utilized, where $d$ represents the bilayer thickness. Solving this expression for $L_z$ yields:

\begin{align}
    &L_z = \frac{A_l N_l}{4\pi R} \\
    &L_x = 2R + \delta\\
    &L_y = 2R + \delta\, ,
\end{align}

where $\delta = 10 \text{r.u.}$ serves as a buffer in the $x$ and $y$ directions to prevent interactions between periodic images. Applying \cref{eq:apl_cylinder} and inserting the outer and inner radii provides the lipid count for both leaflets. \cref{fig:tether_setup} illustrates the resulting cylinder setup.



The subsequent step involves placing the determined number of lipids on a $(\theta,z)$ grid. This process adjusts the orientation of lipids from the \path{POPC-em.gro} file by rotating them $90^\circ$ from the $z$-axis into the $xy$-plane. A subsequent rotation around the grid angle $\theta$ occurs, with an additional $180^\circ$ rotation for lipids depending on their leaflet. The placement of each lipid's center of mass follows

\begin{align}
    \vec{X} = (R_\text{in/out}\cos(\theta), R_\text{in/out}\sin(\theta),z)
\end{align}

for every determined grid pairing in both leaflets. Water molecules fill the space inside and outside the cylinder until the system reaches the target number density. 

The simulations apply the same barostat used in membrane fluctuation measurements to ensure a tension-free bilayer. Following Harmandaris and Deserno \cite{Markus_tethers}, the simulations employ a timestep of $\Delta \hat{t} = 0.005$. Notably, this value exceeds the timestep of the fluctuation measurement, yet the simulation maintains stability. An equilibration phase of $2500 \tau$ relaxes the radius to its equilibrium value \cite{Markus_tethers}, while a subsequent measurement phase of $2500 \tau$ monitors the stress tensor and the radii. Each measurement of the stress tensor is converted into the force by multiplication with the available box area $L_xL_y$. The investigation evaluates cylinders with radii of $6\si{{\nano}{\m}}, 8\si{{\nano}{\m}},$ and $10\si{{\nano}{\m}}$. This ensures a curvature below $90\%$, aligning with the recommendations of Harmandaris and Deserno \cite{Markus_tethers}. 


\chapter{Results}\label{ch:results}

This chapter examines the results and evaluates the calculated values against their MARTINI counterparts. The analysis determines an area per lipid of $0.85\si{\nano\m\squared}$, significantly higher than the MARTINI value of $0.65\si{\nano\m\squared}$. This difference originates from the increased effective size of the DPD beads. Observations indicate that the water-to-lipid ratio primarily affects the system at low values, where it facilitates interactions between periodic images of the membrane.

Bilayer assembly succeeds for systems of 128 and 256 lipids; however, the resulting nematic order parameter remains considerably lower than the MARTINI reference.

Density profiles reveal that the DPD membrane is substantially thinner—approximately $2.9\si{\nano\m}$ depending on system size—and allows for greater penetration of head beads. These properties stem from the softness of the DPD potential, which permits beads to pass through one another without a diverging energy penalty.

The data demonstrates characteristic $k^{-4}$ scaling, and the corresponding fit yields a bending modulus of $\kappa=(5.2\pm0.4)k_\text{B}T$. Similarly to before, this is attributed to the soft interaction of DPD.

\section{Determined Input Parameters}

Measuring the nematic order parameter reveals the bilayer stability for various areas per lipid at $\hat{T}=1.0$ and $\hat{T}=0.8$, as shown in \cref{fig:apl_T_1} and \cref{fig:apl_T_08}. 
Both curves exhibit a comparable shape, characterized by reduced alignment at extreme values  
and a maximum in the intermediate range.
\cref{fig:apl_mol_05}, \cref{fig:apl_mol_09}, and \cref{fig:apl_mol_12} display snapshots representing these three regions. 
A lower area per lipid restricts the available space for the lipids, inducing structural instabilities and preventing distinct phase separation between heads and tails. Conversely, excessive space at elevated areas per lipid triggers the formation of hole defects within the bilayer. The 
membrane at an area per lipid of $0.9\si{\nano\m\squared}$ shows distinct phase separation 
between heads and tails and reaches a degree of alignment characteristic for a fluid lipid bilayer.

Reduced thermal excitation allows the curve at $\hat{T}=0.8$ to attain significantly higher values around the maximum. While the maxima of both curves do not coincide perfectly, both occur near $0.85\si{\nano\m\squared}$. The 128-lipid self-assembly system employs this value, whereas the larger 256-lipid system utilizes an elevated value of $0.87\si{\nano\m\squared}$.

A comparison between the selected area per lipid and the typical values for a POPC MARTINI bilayer $(0.65\si{\nano\m\squared})$ and an all-atom bilayer $(0.684\si{\nano\m\squared})$ \cite{all_atom_popc} reveal significant differences. Examining the respective potentials of both methods clarifies this distinction. The effective bead size, defined as the distance at which the repulsive force ends, strongly influences the area per lipid. In the MARTINI model, the Lennard-Jones potential minimum dictates this size, varying with the specific bead types. For a POPC and water system composed solely of regular sized beads, the $\sigma$ parameter governing the effective size equals $0.47\si{\nano\m}$. Calculating the Lennard-Jones potential minimum yields $1.12\sigma\approx0.53\si{\nano\m}$. Since the DPD potential lacks a minimum, a force analysis identifies the effective bead size as the cutoff value of $0.711\si{\nano\m}$. This discrepancy in the underlying forces and potentials drives the observed difference in area per lipid, as the effective size of MARTINI beads is smaller than the DPD beads. This represents a fundamental distinction between this implementation of CG DPD and MARTINI.

As described in \cref{sec:input_opt}, validating the water-to-lipid ratio remains essential, as the previous simulation runs utilized a constant value of 22. \cref{fig:wpl_T_1} and \cref{fig:wpl_T_08} present the results for bilayer stability at $\hat{T}=1.0$ and $\hat{T}=0.8$.

Both water-to-lipid ratio curves display a similar shape: the order parameter increases as the water-to-lipid ratio rises before leveling off at higher ratios. The large overlap between the error bars highlights this trend.

The additional snapshots in \cref{fig:wpl_mol_8}, \cref{fig:wpl_mol_22}, and \cref{fig:wpl_mol_28} demonstrate that the bilayer collapses at low water-to-lipid ratios, whereas the membranes at ratios of 22 and 28 remain stable. Crucially, fixing the area per lipid implies that a change in the water-to-lipid ratio solely scales the z-axis to maintain the target density of $\rho=3$.

This geometric scaling explains the instabilities observed at low ratios, as the water phase outside the bilayer shields the membrane from interactions with its periodic images. At low ratios, an insufficiently thick water phase permits interactions between the bilayer leaflets across the periodic boundary. This effect accounts for the leveling off of the curve at higher ratios, once the water phase reaches a sufficient thickness, further increases provide no additional stability.

Selecting the lowest water-to-lipid ratio that still ensures sufficient shielding minimizes redundant computational effort from solvent interactions. For the self-assembly of 128 lipids, this criterion results in a ratio of 24, while the 256-lipid system utilizes a ratio of 22.

A high nematic order parameter does not guarantee a perfectly stable or defect-free bilayer. Thus, while the nematic order parameter represents an incomplete metric for evaluating the entire structure, it provides a reliable quick estimate of membrane stability.

Furthermore, since each simulation run occurred only once, the stochastic nature of DPD forces implies that different random seeds may introduce slight variations. Therefore, interpreting minor differences between measured values requires consideration of this inherent variability.

\textbf{Assembly at Lower Temperature}

Testing the bilayer formation at lower temperatures follows a similar protocol, utilizing a target temperature of $\hat{T}=0.8$. \cref{fig:128_low_T_all_info} displays the resulting snapshots alongside the quantitative measurements. The snapshots reveal an evolution similar to \cref{fig:bilayer_form_128_T_1} due to the use of the same production script. However, the finished bilayer appears significantly more ordered and planar.

The nematic order parameter in \cref{fig:128_low_T_nem} confirms this observation, reaching values of 0.7 and significantly exceeding the performance of the $\hat{T}=1.0$ bilayer indicated in the plot. This result aligns with expectations, as reduced thermal energy at lower temperatures restricts random molecular movement, thereby enhancing the stability of the assembled membrane. The significantly reduced noise in the measured values further corroborates this increased stability. Additionally, the contact number rises and eventually matches the values of the MARTINI bilayer. However, even the low, unphysical temperature simulation does not reach the nematic order parameter as MARTINI.

To further probe the structure, the analysis again utilizes density profiles, as shown in \cref{fig:bilayer_128_density_T_08}. While phase separation still lags behind the MARTINI model, the profile demonstrates improvement compared to its higher-temperature counterpart. The head group density nears zero within the apolar phase, indicating that the lower temperature promotes a flatter bilayer and reduces head group penetration into the membrane core.

The bilayer thickness remains reduced at $2.7\si{\nano\m}$ compared to the MARTINI value of $4.1\si{\nano\m}$, which aligns with the results from the higher-temperature simulations.

\section{Bending Modulus by Power Spectrum}

Applying the method from \cref{sec:method_ben_mod_fourier} to DPD and MARTINI bilayers yields two distinct power spectra. \cref{fig:dpd_slope} and \cref{fig:martini_slope} exhibit the characteristic scaling of the power spectrum for both systems. Fitting the data according to \cref{eq:cental_ben_formula_fourier} determines the bending modulus for each membrane. For DPD the bending modulus is $\kappa = (5.2 \pm 0.4)k_\text{B}T$ and for MARTINI $\kappa = (19.0 \pm 0.4)k_\text{B}T$.

All-atom simulations of tension-free POPC bilayers also utilizing the height spectra \cite{all_atom_bending} revealed a value of $(23.3 \pm 1.4)k_\text{B}T$. The reported MARTINI value aligns closely with this result. The DPD value, however, deviates substantially, though closer examination justifies this difference. The softer DPD force permits significantly greater lipid mobility within the membrane, as neighboring lipids do not strongly restrict thermal fluctuations. This is enhanced by the higher area per lipid, as even less friction occurs between the neighboring lipids. Additionally, the DPD membrane is substantially thinner than all-atom or MARTINI membranes. Rawicz et al. \cite{bending_thickness} propose that the bending modulus is dependent on the membrane thickness and thicker membranes lead to higher bending moduli.  

A visual inspection of the membranes during the simulation runs in \cref{fig:ben_dpd_mem} and \cref{fig:ben_martini_mem} confirms this behavior. The DPD membrane exhibits pronounced undulations, which corresponds to a lower bending modulus since deformation requires less force. In contrast, the MARTINI bilayer remains relatively planar, displaying only minor undulations. 

To improve this measurement longer simulations are required as the heavily reduced timesteps leads to much smaller total simulation time. Additionally, each measurement of the height function is only separated by $10\tau$ which does not guarantee decorrelation.

\section{Bending Modulus by Tether Simulation}

\cref{sec:methods_sim_tethers} details the methodology for the tether simulations. \cref{tab:tether_results} illustrates that the measurements deviate from theoretical predictions, as the force increases alongside an expanding radius, contradicting the suggested $F_z \propto 1/R$ scaling in \cref{eq:tether_central_from}. Furthermore, substantial relative errors, exceeding 100\% in one instance, indicate that the force fails to converge correctly. Nevertheless, the bending modulus approximations align closely in scale with the DPD results obtained from membrane fluctuation measurements. The cylinder also maintains stability throughout the simulation, as shown in \cref{fig:equ_cylinder}. 

Due to time constraints, this research does not include further refinements of these measurements. Achieving a converging force measurement requires extended simulation durations for both the equilibration and measurement phases. Harmandaris and Deserno \cite{Markus_tethers} utilized runs ranging from $10000\tau$ to $20000\tau$, which significantly exceed the $2500\tau$ duration applied in this study. Longer simulations are especially beneficial because the stress tensor fluctuates intensely during simulations.

In general, the simulation of large systems with reduced timesteps pose a significant challenge due to increased time needed to run sufficiently long simulations.

\chapter{Conclusion} \label{ch.conclusion}

This thesis presents the membrane self-assembly and bending modlus computation using dissipative particle dynamcis (DPD). Instead of all-atom simulation this coarse-grained DPD model omits atomic detail and utilizes the mapping mechanism of the MARTINI model but employs the soft DPD interaction in favor of the Lennard-Jones potential. This conserves the advantages of coarse-graining while capitalizing on the faster dynamics and integrated thermostat of DPD. Testing the resulting coarse-graining method on a POPC membrane involved simulating self-assembly for multiple system sizes and temperatures, which determined key properties including the area per lipid, the membrane thickness, and the bending modulus. The study computed the latter using a power spectrum analysis and a tensile force measurement \cite{Cooke_bending_mod}. 

The area per lipid and thickness showed clear deviations from the MARTINI and all-atom values. The area per lipid increased because the effective size of the DPD beads exceeded that of MARTINI. The softness of the DPD interaction reduced the bilayer thickness. The difficulty of self-assembly depended strongly on the system size, while smaller membranes assembled without issues, simulations containing larger lipid quantities formed alternative structures and required further intervention to ensure a membrane. Generally, the self-assembly proved successful and computationally efficient, yielding a structure that resembles the MARTINI membrane but poses significantly lower alignment and packing density. The DPD bending modulus also decreased significantly, which stems from a membrane that allows much larger height fluctuations. Additionally, the tensile force measurement proved challenging as the convergence of the force requires long simulation runs. Notably, guaranteeing a tension-free membrane in DPD remains challenging, as the pressure depends strongly on the repulsion parameters and thus proves difficult to interpret in SI units.

Future work could address these deviations from MARTINI and all-atom methods by adjusting the force field. One key aspect involves the cutoff parameter of the DPD force, as it represents the main contributor to the distinct area per lipid. Reducing the cutoff parameter to match the effective size of MARTINI beads could improve comparability between both methods. Crucially, other discrepancies stem from the fundamental formalism of DPD. The softness induces significant molecular mobility, as overlaps entail only minimal energy penalties. Thus, resolving the deviations in thickness and bending modulus in this implementation might prove difficult.
