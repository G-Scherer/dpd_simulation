\chapter{Introduction}

Coarse-grained (CG) molecular dynamic (MD) models are a very 
effective method to simulate large molecular systems. In contrast to all atom simulation 
which resolve individual atomistic trajectories, CG models represent atomic groups
as interaction sites or beads.

The reduced complexity of the system offers substantial acceleration compared to the atomistic 
approach making it possible to simulate much larger systems which are unattainable in all 
atom simulations. The most popular method
for CG molecular dynamics is the MARTINI model with the 
Lennard-Jones potential as the underlying force-field. 

This thesis employs the MARTINI model as a baseline for a system but
then applies a different force-field, namely \textbf{dissipative particle dynamics}
(DPD). To test this approach the self-assembly of 
a lipid bilayer is monitored and afterwards the mechanical properties are determined, more 
specifically the bending modulus.

Lipid bilayers are a fundamental biological structure as they constitute 
the basis of almost all membranes in cells. The polar heads act as a barrier for
any polar or charged particles and molecules thus
making it a very effective structure and worth exploring with DPD.
The chosen lipid 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine 
(POPC) consists of a choline head group which is connected with the 
oleoyl and palmitoyl tails by a phosphate and glycerol group. 

The simulations were carried out using the LAMMPS software which is a
versatile molecular dynamics simulation package.

\chapter{Theory}

\section{Coarse-Grained Simulations}
Coarse-grained simulations \cite{CGwater_review} aim to simulate the behavior of large systems by reducing the 
available degrees of freedom in the system, while still conserving the main thermodynamic and
structural properties. The main method employed is a mapping mechanism that
simplifies groups of atoms into a single interaction vertex or bead. The amount of atoms 
that are coarse-grained differ between different methods and the length scale of interest.

This approach results in the ability to simulate much larger time scales \cite{CGwater_review}. Typical all atom 
approaches only utilize a timestep in the order of femtoseconds \cite{Atomic_timescales}.
This can be enhanced greatly to values in the range of nanoseconds or even microseconds. 
Furthermore, an improvement in computational efficiency comes with decreasing the degrees of freedom. 

The core challenge of a coarse grained simulation is the parametrization of the method. Two main 
methods exist: utilizing empirical data like the outcome of experiments
or the results of more accurate all atom simulations to parametrize the coarse-grained interactions.
Not every thermodynamic property can be replicated in coarse-grained system, thus 
the method is chosen to reproduce the desired properties \cite{CGwater_review}.

This work employs the mapping method of the MARTINI method \cite{Martini}, the details of which are explained in 
\cref{martini}. 


\section{The MARTINI Model}\label{martini}

The MARTINI model \cite{Martini} is one of the most widely used methods for coarse-grained simulations.
Central to this approach is the representation of different groups of atoms, 
MARTINI distinguishes between five basic bead 
types: water (W), polar (P), non-polar (N), apolar (C), charged (Q).
The P and C beads are also assigned a number from 1 to 5 depending on the 
polarity, reaching from P1 being the least polar and P5 being the most
polar whereas C1 is the most apolar and C5 the least apolar.
The W bead is identical to the P4 bead. The Q and N beads can also have 
subtypes which quantify the hydrogen bond forming capabilities.
There is 0 meaning no capabilities, d meaning some donor capabilities and 
a meaning some acceptor capabilities.  

While the MARTINI scheme usually utilizes the Lennard-Jones potential and the corresponding
force for non-bonded beads, in this work another force field, explained in \cref{dpd}
is employed while adapting the same 
mapping mechanism of the MARTINI method. 

\section{Dissipative Particle Dynamics} \label{dpd}
The DPD force field was first presented by Groot and Warren in their work on simulating systems on 
the mesoscopic scale \cite{DPD_groot_warren} and is widely used in simulations of fluids.
The equations of motion for a particle $i$ influenced by all other particles $j$
in a DPD force field is governed by

\begin{equation}
    m \frac{\text{d}\mathbf{v}_i}{\text{d}t} = \sum_{j \neq i} \mathbf{F}^C_{ij} + 
    \mathbf{F}^D_{ij} + \mathbf{F}^R_{ij}\, ,  
\end{equation}

containing three different parts, more specifically the conservative
force $\mathbf{F}^C$, the dissipative force $\mathbf{F}^D$ and the random force $\mathbf{F}^R$. 

The conservative force can be expressed as

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{F}^C_{ij} = \begin{cases}
        A_{ij}\left (1 - \frac{r_{ij}}{r_c} \right )\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}, & r_{ij} \leq r_c \\
        0, & r_{ij} > r_c 
    \end{cases}
    \, ,
\end{equation}

where $r_c$ is a cutoff distance and $A_{ij}$ is
a particle pair specific repulsion parameter. Furthermore,
$\mathbf{r}_{ij} = \mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j$ defines the distance vector,
with $r_{ij} = |\mathbf{r}_{ij}|$ being its magnitude 
and $\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij} = \mathbf{r}_{ij}/r_{ij}$ the corresponding unit vector.

Usually the cutoff distance is set to $r_c = 1$ when working with reduced units, which will be 
discussed in \cref{dpd_units}, 
making it redundant in the formula above. Both force fields are presented in \cref{comp_forces} 
to further illustrate the difference between the MARTINI method 
in \cref{martini} with the Lennard-Jones potential parametrized by the length scale $\sigma$ and the
energy scale $\epsilon$
\begin{equation}
    U^{LJ} = 4 \epsilon \left [\left(\frac{\sigma}{r} \right)^{12} 
    - \left(\frac{\sigma}{r} \right)^6 \right ]\,  \label{LJ_pot}
\end{equation}

and the previously defined DPD force.
The key difference between both 
approaches is that the DPD force and the corresponding potential
do not diverge at $r=0$ which means that overlapping particles are allowed
making it a soft potential. Adding to this DPD is a purely repulsive 
force whereas Lennard-Jones has a clear attractive part and a minimum.
Furthermore the DPD force is a short range interaction, whereas the Lennard-Jones force 
has theoretically unlimited range but is usually truncated to accelerate simulations.

The dissipative and random force can best be described by using two weight 
functions $w^D$ and $w^R$. Using the work of Espanol and Warren \cite{Espanol_warren}
one weight function can be arbitrarily chosen but in turn fixes the other one. 
The usual choice for the weighting funciton yields

\begin{equation}
    w^D(r_{ij}) = \left [w^R(r_{ij}) \right ]^2 = \begin{cases}
        \left (1 - \frac{r_{ij}}{r_c} \right )^2, & r_{ij} \leq r_c \\
        0, & r_{ij} > r_c
    \end{cases}\, .
\end{equation}

Both forces are controlled by strength coefficients, namely $\sigma$ for the random force
and $\gamma$ for the drag force. The fluctuation dissipation theorem connects these
two parameters with the thermal Energy $k_B T$ and yields

\begin{equation}
    \sigma^2 = 2 \gamma k_B T. \label{connection_fluc_dis}
\end{equation}

Defining $\mathbf{v}_{ij} = \mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{v}_j$ as the
relative velocity between two particles
and $\alpha_{ij}$ as 
a random number with zero mean and unit variance 
results in a final expression for both forces

\begin{align}
    \mathbf{F}^D_{ij} &= -\gamma w^D(r_{ij})(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}\cdot \mathbf{v}_{ij})
    \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij} \\
    \mathbf{F}^R_{ij} &= \sigma w^R(r_{ij}) \alpha_{ij} \Delta t^{-1/2} \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}\, .
\end{align}

As mentioned both forces are deeply connected through the fluctuation dissipation theorem with 
\cref{connection_fluc_dis} meaning that the amount of energy added through the random force
$\mathbf{F}^R$ 
is balanced by the dissipative force $\mathbf{F}^D$. Consequently, both forces act as internal 
thermostat of the system allowing sampling of the canonical ensemble and eliminates the need for any
external thermostats during simulation.

Notably a factor of $\Delta t^{-1/2}$ appears in the random force. This correction
follows the argument of Groot and Warren \cite{DPD_groot_warren} as this 
corrects the time dependency of displacement to the known expression 
$\left < x^2\right >\propto t$.

To fully describe DPD interaction, the only parameters to determine are the 
repulsion parameters $A_{ij}$ and the drag coefficient $\gamma$ which in turn fixes the 
random force parameter $\sigma$. This step will be further discussed in \cref{sec:dpd_parameters}.

\section{Electrostatic Interaction}\label{coulomb}

The DPD force in \cref{dpd} does not differentiate between a charged or an uncharged bead, meaning 
some kind of coulomb interaction has to be added to the force field, 
or the repulsion have to be adapted correspondingly. Crucially a simple 
coulomb potential is an unfitting choice because it would destroy the soft property of the
potential at $r=0$. To solve this 
a exponentially smeared charge distribution resulting in a Slater potential is employed 
. This is following the work of Wang and Hernandez \cite{Wang2024}
who also introduce a factor $\beta$ which controls the strength of the
smearing effect. The charge distribution then reads

\begin{equation}
    \rho(r) = \frac{q\beta^2}{\pi r} \exp(-2\beta r)\, . \label{charge_density}
\end{equation}

Utilizing a conversion constant $C$ the coulomb interaction follows

\begin{align}
    \mathbf{F}^E_{ij} = \frac{C q_i q_j}{4 \pi r_{ij}}[1 - \exp(-2\beta r_{ij})
    (1+2\beta r_{ij}(1+\beta r_{ij}))]\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}\, . \label{coulomb_force}
\end{align}


\section{Intramolecular Interactions}\label{intermol}

Beyond the non-bonded interacitons defined in \cref{dpd} and \cref{coulomb}, intramolecular potentials
between different number of participating beads ensure the structural integrity of the moldecules \cite{Martini}.
Interaction between two beads are defined as bonds and determine the equilibrium distance
between two beads,
whereas angle potentials control the local geometry between three consecutive beads. 
For four body interaction 
a dihedral interaction is utilized characterizing the torsion around a central bond, convserving 
properties like the orientation of specific atomic groups. Lastly the improper interaction 
maintain the chriality or planarity of molecular groups.

The employed potential depends on the molecule that is represented and not every molecle utilizes every
kind of interaction. More explicit application of this will be discussed in \cref{topology}.

\section{Numerical Integration}

The forces defined in \cref{dpd}, \cref{coulomb} and \cref{intermol} yield a system of
ordinary differential equations for 
the system that must be solved in molecular dynamics simulations. Therefore 
numerical integrators are employed which rely on the discretization of time, partitoning it into 
timesteps of the size $\Delta t$. The standard method for LAMMPS is the velocity verlet integrator \cite{Allen2017_verlet} which
calculates the desired quantities of a bead at the new timestep $t+ \Delta t$ using the former timestep 
$t$. The procedure repeated every timestep is

\begin{alignat}{2}
    &1.\ \text{Predict half step velocity} \quad &&\mathbf{v}(t + \Delta t/2) = \mathbf{v}(t) + 
    \mathbf{a}(t) \Delta t/2\\
    &2.\ \text{Update positions} \quad&&\mathbf{x}(t + \Delta t) = \mathbf{x}(t) + 
    \mathbf{v}(t + \Delta t/2)\Delta t\\
    &3.\ \text{Calculate new acceleration} \quad&&\mathbf{a}(t + \Delta t) = 
    \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}(t + \Delta t), \mathbf{v}(t + \Delta t/2))/m\\
    &4.\ \text{Correct final velocity} \quad&&\mathbf{v}(t + \Delta t) = 
    \mathbf{v}(t + \Delta t/2) + \mathbf{a}(t + \Delta t)\Delta t/2\, .
\end{alignat}

This version of verlet differentiates itself from other methods by utilizing the velocity of the beads 
at an intermediate timestep $t+\Delta t/2$ which is central for the DPD force as the dissipative force 
depends on the velocity. This scheme \cite{Allen2017_verlet} is numerically stable 
and conserves energy even for large timesteps. 


\section{Periodic Boundary Condition}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=14cm]{figures/theory/pbc.png}
    \caption{Visualization of the periodic boundary condition in LAMMPS. The left shows a single 
    simulation cell in which the interactions are calculated. On the right is the resulting 
    system after employing the periodic boundary condition with interactions beyond the single cell boundaries.}
    \label{fig:pbc}

Computer simulation face the challenge of only being able to simulate finite systems. This 
limitation leads to unphysical surface effects when particles reach the boundary of the system. 
Numerous possibilites exist to mitigate this
but the standard meachnism is to enforce a periodic boundary condition (PBC) \cite{LammpsBoundary}. Employing a 
PBC means that a particle leaving the simulation box re-enters simultaneously enters
the box from the opposing boundary. Furthermore 
a particle near a boundary also interacts with the particles on the other side of the box, utilizing the nearest
image convention, meaning that any particle only interact with the closest image of each neighboring particle. 
This leads to 
a system consisting of an infinfite grid of the same finite system. The effect of the PBC is 
visualized in \cref{fig:pbc}.

\section{Assembly parameters}\label{nematic}

The degree of order in a system is of high importance when simulating self-assembly processes as 
it quantifies the macroscopic state of the system. The nematic order parameter 
\cite{Ramasubramani2020_freud}, \cite{FreudNematicDoc} measures 
how much particles align. The parameter is derived from the traceless second-order
tensor $\mathbf{Q}$, defined as 

\begin{align}
    \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{M} - \frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\, ,
\end{align}

where $\mathbf{M}$ describes the particle orientation and  $\mathbf{I}$ is the unit matrix.
The definition of $\mathbf{M}$ utilizes the normalized orientation vecotor $\mathbf{m}^{(i)}$ 
of the particle $i$ and reads

\begin{align}
    \mathbf{M}_{\alpha \beta} = \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{m}_\alpha^{(i)}\mathbf{m}_\beta^{(i)}\, .
\end{align}

The eigenvalues of $\mathbf{Q}$ are $\frac{2}{3}S$, $-\frac{1}{3}S$ and $-\frac{1}{3}S$, where $S$ is 
the desired order parameter. The corresponding eigenvector of the largest eigenvector is called the 
director and measures the principal alignment of the system. 

In addition to the measurement of alignment in the system, the packing density of the system can 
be quantified to ensure that the lipids form a connected structure. To quantify this a 
contact number is defined as the average number of neighboring lipids. A lipid is 
classified as a neighbor if the distance is smaller than a specified cutoff distance $r_c$. 

\section{Membrane Fluctuations}

In continoiums theory the energy of an almost flat membrane \cite{Cooke_bending_mod} can be expressed as 

\begin{align}
    E = \frac{1}{2} \int \mathrm d\mathbf x \left[ 
        \kappa (\Delta h(\mathbf x))^2 + \Sigma (\nabla h(\mathbf x))^2 \right]\, , \label{eq:mem_energy}
\end{align}

where $h(\mathbf x)$ is the two dimensional height function of the membrane. To further investigate
the membrane \cite{Schwarz2024}
the height function can be expressed 
as the inverse of the fourier transform 

\begin{align}
    h(\mathbf x) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)} \int \mathrm d\mathbf k 
    \tilde{h}(\mathbf k) \exp(i\mathbf k \cdot \mathbf x)\, , 
\end{align}

whith the wave vector $\mathbf k$. It is instructive to introduce 
the dirac delta distribution represented in fourier space 

\begin{align}
    (2\pi)^2 \delta(\mathbf k) = \int \mathrm d \mathbf x \exp(i\mathbf k \cdot \mathbf x)\, .
\end{align}

Crucially, the height function is purely real, meaning $h(\mathbf x) = h(\mathbf x)^*$ leading to 
the relation in fourier space $h(\mathbf k)^* = h(-\mathbf k)$. Inserting the results into 
\cref{eq:mem_energy} yields 

\begin{align}
    E = \frac{1}{2} \int \mathrm d \mathbf k 
    |\tilde{h}(\mathbf k)|^2 \left[\kappa k^4 + \Sigma k^2 \right]\, .
\end{align}

The equipartition theorem connects the average of each mode squared $\langle |\tilde{h}
(\mathbf k)|^2\rangle$ with the thermal energy of $k_{\mathrm B} T/2 $ leading to the 
expression 

\begin{align}
    \langle |\tilde{h}(\mathbf k)|^2\rangle = \frac{k_{\mathrm B}T}{\kappa k^4 + \Sigma k^2}\, .
\end{align}

Instead of the continuous fourier transform a discrete fourier transform

\begin{align}
    h(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)}\sum_\mathbf{k} \tilde{h}_\mathbf{k} \exp(i \mathbf k \cdot \mathbf x) 
\end{align}

is typically employed whith the discrete wave vectors $\mathbf k = 2\pi/L\cdot (n_x,n_y)$.
Repeating the calculation for a membrane of the area $A = L^2$ leads to the integral 

\begin{align}
    \int_A \mathrm d\mathbf x \exp(i\mathbf x \cdot(\mathbf k + \mathbf {k'})) = 
    L^2 \delta_{\mathbf k , -\mathbf{k'} }\, ,  
\end{align}

which differs from the continuous calculation only in a factor $L^2$ and substituting the 
dirac delta function for a Kronecker delta. Dimensionality analysis further confirms this result 
as the dimension of $[\delta(\mathbf k + \mathbf{k'})] = [L^2 \delta_{\mathbf k , -\mathbf{k'} }] = m^2$
match. This leads to a slighty different result for a discrete fourier tansformation

\begin{align}
    \langle |\tilde{h}_\mathbf{k}|^2\rangle = \frac{k_{\mathrm B}T}{L^2(\kappa k^4 + \Sigma k^2)}\, .
\end{align}






\chapter{Methods}

\section{System Topology}\label{topology}

The topology of the molecule is defined in the \path{martini_v_2.0_POPC.itp} file 
obtained from the MARTINI website. It serves as the baseline for the 
parametrization in the following sections. It lists the employed potential for the 
bonds and angles, including their equilibrium parameters in MARTINI units, as well
as the bead types used in the representation.

\cref{martini} introduced the basic bead types in the MARTINI method. In this work the system consists 
of POPC lipids and water molecules acting as the solvent. Each water bead is a P4 bead and 
contains four classical water molecules. 

The POPC lipids consist of a choline head group (Q0, 1), a phosphate group (Qa, 2) and two 
glycerol groups (Na, 3/4) connecting the polar part to two tails. These are made up of a oleoyl (C1, 5/6/8; C3, 7)
and a palmitoyl group (C1, 9/10/11/12) and are highly apolar. Each bead is assigned an ID ranging from
one to twelve to precicely reference one bead. The resulting molecule consiting of 
the five unique bead types and twelve total beads
is shown in \cref{fig:popc_lammps}. The relative position of the beads is based on MARTINI and extraced from the 
\path{POPC-em.gro} file which contains the positions of each bead in one molecule. The beads are connected by eleven bonds and eight angles, with 
two unique bond types and three angle types. The specific bonds and angles are described in \cref{tab:bond_angle_connections}.
There are no dihedral or improper interactions in the POPC molecule.

\cref{intermol} defined the interaction between beads inside a molecule. For the bond interaction the potential is a 
simple harmonic potential, which is implemented 
in LAMMPS as

\begin{equation}
    E_{ij} = \hat{K}^b(r_{ij} - r^0_{ij})^2. \label{harmonic}\, ,
\end{equation}

where $K_b$ encodes the bond stiffness, containing the usual 
factor of $1/2$ and $r^0_{ij}$ is the equilibrium
bond distance of the bead pair. This works similarly for the angle using the potential

\begin{equation}
    E_{ijk} = \hat{K}^a[\cos(\theta_{ijk} - \cos(\theta^0_{ijk}))]^2\, , \label{cosine/squared}
\end{equation}


with $K_a$ fulfills the identical role as $K_b$ and similarly
to before $\theta^0_{ijk}$ the equilibrium angle between the three beads. Though the choice 
is given by the MARTINI database it is interesting to ask why potential of choice is 
not simply harmonic for the angles. Mainly, the potential in \cref{cosine/squared}
is much more computationally efficient because the calculation of a cosine does not include the $\arccos(\theta)$
function, which is expensive and numerically sensitive.
A detailed calculation of the equilibrium parameters is done in \cref{sec:dpd_bond_params}.

\section{DPD Units}\label{dpd_units}

Molecular dynamics simulations frequently employ reduced units,
which utilizes reference parameters to render every physical quantity
dimensionless. For MARTINI these are usually the parameters of the Lennard-Jones potential
$\epsilon$ and $\sigma$ as in \cref{LJ_pot}. However, since DPD has a different 
force and thus potential one needs different reference parameters. For the 
length parameter, the work of Wang and Hernandez (\textbf{cite here}) is followed. They
defined the length scale based on the volume required to contain three CG water beads. This 
choice is not arbitrary as it 
follows the early
work of Groot and Warren 
(\textbf{cite here}) who established the number density $\rho=3$ DPD beads per unit of volume $V = r_{\text{ref}}^3$. 
In MARTINI every water bead contains $N_w = 4$ molecules and
the volume of one water molecule $V_w$ can be calculated by
using the molar mass $M=18.02 \si{\g\per\mol}$,
the density $\rho_w = 1 \si{\g\per\cubic\centi\meter}$ and the avogadro
constant $N_A = 6.022 \cdot 10^{23} 
\text{molecules}/\text{mol}$

\begin{equation}
    V_w = \frac{M}{\rho_w N_A} = 2.99 \cdot 10^{-23} \si{\cubic\centi\m} 
    = 29.9 \si{\cubic\angstrom}\, .
\end{equation}

The DPD length follows directly

\begin{equation}
    r_{\text{ref}} = \sqrt[3]{\rho \cdot N_w \cdot V_w} = 7.11 \si{\angstrom}\, ,
\end{equation}

which from now on can be used to convert every length into DPD units by division with the 
reference length.

The definition of an energy scale is much simpler. The reference temperature was defined as
$T_{\text{ref}}=298.15 \si{\K}$ and using the thermal energy as the reference unit is 

\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_{\text{ref}} = k_{\mathrm{B}} T_{\text{ref}} = 4.12 \cdot 10^{-21} \si{\J} = 
    25.7 \si{\milli\eV}\, .
\end{equation}

Similarly to the length scale this energy scale allows conversion of every energy value to 
be converted into DPD units by division. Furthermore, the combination of both scales can be 
utilized to make every physical quantity dimensionless. 

Since this work aims to investigate dynamic processes, namely the self-assembly it is cruicial
to introduce a timescale. Usually this entails a simple composition of the 
previously introduce scales but for DPD this has to be handled with more care. 
Following the work of Wang and Hernandez \textbf{cite here} the timescale is calculated by 
matching the diffusion constant of DPD water to the experimental value. This leads to 
a timescale of 

\begin{align}
    \tau = 91.4\si{\pico\s}\, .
\end{align}


\section{Determination of DPD Bonded Parameters}\label{sec:dpd_bond_params}

As detailed in \cref{topology} the equilibrium parameters are sourced from the \path{martini_v_2.0_POPC.itp}
file. They are given in SI units by the MARTINI database, specifically the unit for distances is 
$[r] = \si{\nano\m}$ and for the bond stiffness $[k^b] = \si{\kilo\J\per\mol\per\nano\m\squared}$.
Similarly the angle stiffness is given in $[k^a] = \si{\kilo\J\per\mol}$ as the angles 
themselves are already dimensionless. 

For the conversion into DPD unists the reference scales defined in \cref{dpd_units} are utilized. 
Besides the application of the scales, the MARTINI units have to be divided by the avogadro constant as 
they are given in molar units.
The bond spring 
constant in DPD unit, $\hat{K}^b$, is calculates as

\begin{align}
    \hat{K}^b = \frac{1}{2} k^b \frac{r_{\text{ref}}^2}{e_{\text{ref}}N_A}\, , \label{eq:bond_spring_conversion}
\end{align}

where $k^b$ is the equilibrium force constant from MARTINI with the factor $1/2$ discussed in \cref{topology}. 
The conversion of the equilibrium distinances 
is simply 

\begin{align}
    \hat{r}^0 = r^0/r_{\text{ref}}\, .
\end{align}

The angle spring constant follows the same pattern as \cref{eq:bond_spring_conversion}, 
yielding

\begin{align}
    \hat{K}^a = \frac{1}{2}  \frac{k^a}{e_{\text{ref}}N_A}\, ,
\end{align}

with the MARTINI value $k^a$. The POPC molecule utilizes multiple bond and angle types, as defined 
in \cref{topology}. The converted values, alongside their MARTINI  counterparts for each type 
are summarized in \cref{tab:bond_angle_params}. The shown values are used in the simulations.

\section{Determination of DPD Charge}

As introduced in \cref{coulomb} the coulombic interaction for the beads is modeled using a slater potential, 
as detailed in \cref{charge_density} and \cref{coulomb_force}. It employs a strength parameter and
based on earlier works from Gonzales-Melchor et al (\textbf{cite here})
this factor $\beta$ and its inverse $\lambda$, utilized in the LAMMPS implementation 
of the potential were determined to be

    
\begin{align}
    \beta = 0.844 = 1/\lambda = 1/1.185\, .
\end{align}
    
The introduction of a length and energy scale also has an effect on the charge scaling. To adjust 
the charge, the potential energy of two point charges in SI units is equated
with the corresponding energy in DPD units 
yielding 

\begin{align}
    &\frac{q^2}{4\pi \epsilon_0 r}  = 
    \frac{\hat{q}^2}{\hat{r}} e_{\text{ref}}\\
    &\hat{q} = \frac{q}{\sqrt{4\pi \epsilon_0 r_{\text{ref}}e_{\text{ref}}}} = 8.86\, .
\end{align}

Defining this charge means that a charge of one elemental charge in SI units corresponds 
to a dimensionless DPD charge of $\hat{q} = 8.86$. The additional factors of $4\pi\epsilon_0$ are also absorbed 
in the charge definition as is standard in LAMMPS.

\section{Determination of DPD Force Parameters}\label{sec:dpd_parameters}

The repulsion parameters $A_{ij}$ for the DPD force are the core of the force field,
determining the maximum repulsion of the conservative force defined in \cref{dpd}.
In addition to that the drag parameter $\gamma$ has to be determined.
The latter was derived from the early work of Groot and Warren (\textbf{cite here}) who
found at $\hat{T} =1$ the optimal value for the random force parameter was $\hat{\sigma} = 3$.
Employing the connection between both parameters given by the fluctuation dissipation thoerem and 
setting $k_{text{b}}T$ to unity yields
 
\begin{align}
    \hat{\gamma} = 4.5\, .
\end{align}

The drag parameter $\hat{\gamma}$ is a fix parameter in the DPD 
force field implementation in the LAMMPS package. Changing the target temperature of the DPD force 
adjusts the random force parameter $\hat{\sigma}$ according to the fluctuation dissipation theorem.

The repulsion parameters used in the simulation were derived by Wang and Hernandez (\textbf{cite here}).
The values are shown in \cref{tab:dpd_params}. The derivation method is described below.

The baseline is the repulsion parameter between two water beads W-W, called $A_{ww}$. 
This is done by matching the inverse compressibility $\kappa^{-1}$ from experimental data 
to the corresponding value of $A_{ww}$. The discovered value is 

\begin{align}
    A_{ww} = 102.067\, .
\end{align}

In MARTINI model the effective Lennard-Jones radius
$\sigma_{ww}$ parameter of W-W interaction is used for every other same bead, meaning 
$\sigma_{AA} = \sigma_{ww}$. Analogous to this the discovered repulsion parameter $A_{ww}$ is 
also chosen for every other same bead repulsion $A_{AA} = A_{ww}$ for all bead types $A$.

From there the interaction of two arbitrary unlike beads ($A \neq B$) beads can be expressed as 

\begin{equation}
    A_{AB} = A_{ww} + \Delta A_{AB}\, .
\end{equation}

The parameter $\Delta A_{AB}$ can be expressed, using the Flory-Huggins parameter as 

\begin{equation}
    \chi_{AB} = \lambda_\chi \Delta A_{AB}\, ,
\end{equation}

using a fitting parameter $\lambda_\chi$ that connects both models.
Earlier works by Groot and Rabone (\textbf{cite here}) indicate that the Flory-Huggins 
parameter $\chi_{AB}$ scales linear with the size of the beads which in this system is always $N_m = 4$ 
molecules per bead, implying 

\begin{equation}
    \chi_{AB} = \bar{\chi}_{AB} N_m\, .
\end{equation}

This results in a final formula for the interaction parameter

\begin{equation}
    A_{AB} = A_{ww} + \bar{\chi}_{AB} N_m/\lambda_\chi\, . \label{final_form_dpd}
\end{equation}

To determine the $\lambda$ fitting parameter a phase seperation simulation is 
conducted to using a box initially filled with two different bead types $A$ and $B$.
After equilibration the volume fraction is measured. This method is repeated for 
different values of $\Delta a_{AB}$. For each simulation run the Flory-Huggins paremter is 
calculated using the measured equilibrium volume fraction $\phi$

\begin{equation}
    \chi = \frac{\ln[(1-\phi)/\phi]}{1 - 2\phi}
\end{equation}

and plotted against the parameter $\Delta A_{AB}$. The slope of the resulting linear fit 
yields the fitting parameter 

\begin{align}
    \lambda_\chi = 0.28\, .
\end{align}

Wang and Hernandez (\textbf{cite here}) employed a formula which uses a baseline interaction matrix
$\epsilon_{AB}$ to calculate the size dependant Flory-Hugigns parameter.

\begin{equation}
    \bar{\chi}_{AB}N_m = \chi_{AB} = \frac{z}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T} \left[\epsilon_{AB} - 
    \frac{1}{2} (\epsilon_{AA} + \epsilon_{BB}) \right] \, ,
\end{equation}

using $z$ as the effective number of nearest neighbors.
The employed database 
is the MARTINI model. The calculated values can be substituted into \cref{final_form_dpd}. 
The final values in \cref{tab:dpd_params} are used in this work for every 
simulation.

\section{System Setup}\label{sec:sys_setup}

Initiating a simulation in LAMMPS requires an input files containing a header that specifies
the number of beads, number of bond/angle/dihedral/improper types (\cref{intermol}),
box dimensions and bead masses. Additionally, an atoms section, defining the position of every bead, 
a bonds section, defining which beads 
are connected by bonds and an angles section, defining which beads are connected by angles. Lastly
there is a section
containing the equilibrium parameters of the bonds and angle types.
Usually, there are also sections for dihedral and improper interactions but, as mentioned, for the system 
in this work they can be omitted.

To generate this file a python script is utilized which takes as input: the number of lipids in the system $N_l$,
the number density $\rho$,
the area per lipid $A_l$ and the water to lipid ratio $R_{w/l} = N_w/N_l$, where $N_w$ is the 
amount of water beads in the system. The area per lipid is defined as the available area in 
a square bilayer configuration, meaning 

\begin{align}
    A_l = \frac{L_x L_y}{N_l/2}\, , \label{eq:def_apl}
\end{align}

for a supposed bilayer in the $xy$-plane with dimensions $L_x$ and $L_y$. The number density 
is a set parameter following the work of Groot and Warren (\textbf{cite here}) with $\rho=3$.
The other two inputs, the area per lipid $A_l$ and water to lipid ratio $R_{w/l}$ 
are examined in detail in the following
sections.

The header information 
number of bead, bond, angle, improper and dihedral type was already discussed in \cref{topology} and \cref{tab:bond_angle_connections}.
In a system of POPC molecules with water this yields: 6 bead types, 2 bond types and 3 angle types. 

The total amount of beads, bonds and angles are calculated based on the number of bonds and angles per 
molecule described in \cref{tab:bond_angle_connections} and the input parameters $N_l$ and $R_{w/l}$

\begin{align}
    &N(\text{beads}) = (12 + R_{w/l}) \cdot N_l \label{eq:num_beads}\\ 
    &N(\text{bonds}) = 11 \cdot N_l\\
    &N(\text{angles}) = 8 \cdot N_l\, .
\end{align}

The box dimensions, introduced in \cref{eq:def_apl}, are calculated by 
utilizing the area per lipid, the number density and the total number of beads in the system
calculated in \cref{eq:num_beads}

\begin{align}
    V &= \frac{N(\text{beads})}{\rho}\\
    L_x = L_y &= \sqrt{N_l/2 \cdot A_l}\\
    L_z &= \frac{V}{L_x L_y}\, .
\end{align}

The masses for each bead are set to unity in DPD units, $\hat{m} = 1$,
completing the information needed in the header.

The information for the bonds and angles section are taken from \cref{tab:bond_angle_connections}
for every POPC molecule. Similarly, the equilibrium parameters are shwon in \cref{tab:bond_angle_params}
and can be utilized directly. 

The remaining atom section sets the starting position of each bead 
and is dependant on the target configuration and will be described in the following sections.

\section{Optimization of Input Parameters}\label{sec:input_opt}

Before the self-assembly process can be investigated, the remaining input paramters must 
be optimized. The optimal approach to determine the parameters would be to test every 
combination of area per lipid $A_l$ and water to lipid ratio $R_{w/l}$ resulting in a grid search, where 
the global maximum is the optimal parameter pairing. This is a computationally expensive approach,
requiring the simulation of potentially irrelevant combinations. To circumvent this procedure an 
initial guess for the water to lipid ratio is employed: $R_{w/l}^{\text{init}} \approx 22$.   
Holding the inital water to lipid ratio and the fixed number density 
numerous simulations are performed on a pre-assembled bilayer configuration consisting of 128 lipids,
varying the area per lipid.

The pre-assembled bilayer is generated by configuring the atoms section 
described in \cref{sec:sys_setup}.
The \path{POPC-em.gro} file is used as a baseline for each molecule topology and each molecule 
is placed on one of two grids in the $xy$-planes, seperated by a fixed value.
The lateral spacing of the grid points is 
determined by the target area per lipid. To orient the tails inwards, one leaflet in rotated by 180° 
degrees around the $x$-axis. To complete the atoms section water beads are placed outside of
the leaflets to act as the solvent in the system.

With this setup 15 simulations were carried out with 128 POPC lipids, an inital water to lipid ratio of $R_{w/l} = 22$ 
with varying area per lipids ranging from $A_l = 0.5 \si{\nano\m\squared}$ to $A_l = 1.2 \si{\nano\m\squared}$
at a temperature of $\hat{T} = 1.0$ and $\hat{T} = 0.8$. The significance of the temperature choice will be investigated in the 
subsequent sections. Each simulation run was $3000 \tau$ DPD timesteps with a timestep $\Delta \hat{t} = 0.01$ 
following Wang and Hernandez (\textbf{cite here}). To quantify the quality of the bilayer the 
nematic order parameter introduced in \cref{nematic} was utilized and calculted for the last $2000 \tau$
DPD timesteps to discard effects from the grid starting position. To calculte the 
order parameter the normalized head-to-tail vector was utilized as orientation. 

The next step involves investigating the water to lipid ratio and ensure that the initial guess 
was justified. The procedure is the same as before, but instead of varying the area per lipid, 
the water to lipid ratio is varied with the area per lipid set to the best value of the previous 
simulations. With the optimal pairing the self-assembly process can be started.

\section{Spontanious Self-Assembly Protocol} \label{sec:128_assembly}

To simulate self-assembly the lipid and water molecules are dispersed randomly throughout the simulation 
box. To achieve this the atom section is adjusted to display a disordered configuration (lipid soup)
instead of the pre-assembled bilayer formation 
in the former section. Each lipid and water molecule is assigned a random center of mass. 
To achieve random allignment of the lipids a rotation around every axis is added to each individual
lipid. After these displacements the periodic boundary condition is applied to keep every bead inside 
the designated box volume. 

Equilibrating the system is achieved by running it a an elevated temperature of $\hat{T} = 2$
where no self-assembly occurs. To prevent large tempreature temperature fluctuations
at the start of the simulation, every atom is assigned a random velocity 
governed by the temperature and a gaussian distribution. To induce bilayer formation the temperature is 
gradually lowered to $\hat{T} = 1$ by changing 
the temperature argument of the DPD force. Doing so does not allow to control the speed of 
the temperature change as the DPD thermostat adjusts the random force strength parameter $\hat{sigma}$
instantaneously, via $\hat{\sigma} = \sqrt{2 \hat{\gamma}\hat{T}}$. To emulate a continous ramping of 
the temperature 
a loop is employed which changes the target 
DPD temperature every few steps. 
After reaching the target tempreature value of $\hat{T} = 1$, a longer production phase
is excecuted to stabilize the structure.
To investigate the effect of lower temperatures on the quality of the final structure 
the procedure is repeated using a production
temperature of $\hat{T} = 0.8$.

To quantify the bilayer structure the nemaitc order parameter and the contact number from \cref{nematic}
are utilized to measure both the allignment and the packing density of the bilayer. Furthermore, a
comparison to the MARTINI model is added, by calculating the same metrics for a pre-assembled and 
equilibrated MARTINI bilayer at $T = 300\si{K}$. To test whether the bilayer reached the 
target structure  
a pre-assembled DPD bilayer at both temperatures is also compared. 
Additionally, to investigate the bead distribution a density profile along the
bilayer axis is analyzed. The beads are split into three groups: head group (ID 1,2,3,4)
, first tail group (ID 5,6,9,10)  
and the terminal tail group (ID 7,8,11,12). The IDs can be compared to \cref{fig:popc_lammps}
for visualization.


\section{Implementation of Larger Systems}\label{sec:larger_systems}

To test the effect of the amount of lipids, a system containing double the amount of lipids at 256
is utilized. The setup is similar to \cref{sec:128_assembly} but since the area per lipid and 
the water to lipid ratio were optimized for 128 lipids the paramers for 256 lipids are finetuned 
for this system. This entails slight changes to the area per lipid and the water to lipid ratio.

The simulation procedure starts at $\hat{T} = 2$ with an 
identical velocity initialization. A longer 
equilibration run was necessary to prevent ensure proper dispersion and prevent 
unwanted clumping of lipid tails. The cooling 
process commences until the system reaches the target temperature of $\hat{T} = 1$.
This initial configuration resulted in bilayer formation within a non-cubic area where 
the area per lipid is below the optimal value.
To achive  a square-shaped bilayer the box is 
gradually deformed to adjust the dimenstions while retining the same box volume.   
After the deformation process the production
phase begins. The time of simulation is increased for this system due to the increased size.

The investigation of the bilayer quality is identical to \cref{sec:128_assembly} with the same 
MARTINI bilayer but DPD bilayers at the same system size. 

\chapter{Results}

\section{Determined input parameters}

Bilayer stability for different area per lipids at $\hat{T} = 1.0$ and 
$\hat{T} = 0.8$ is analysed by measurement of the nematic order parameter and is shown  
in \cref{fig:apl_T_1} and \cref{fig:apl_T_08}. 
Both curves show a similar shape with lower order at high and low areas per lipid 
and a maximum at intermediate values.
To further illustrate this, snapshots of the bilayers with area per lipid in all 
three regions are shown in \cref{fig:apl_mol_05}, \cref{fig:apl_mol_09} and \cref{fig:apl_mol_12}. 
It is apparent that a lower area per lipid leads to a high repulsion between the lipids 
destroying the bilayer structure. This is evident by the fact that there is no clean 
phase seperation between the heads and the tails. For an elevated area per lipid there 
is too much space which leads to the formation of hole defects in the bilayer. The 
bilayer at an area per lipid of $0.9\si{\nano\m\squared}$ shows a clean phase seperation 
between heads and tails and reaches a good alignment for a fluid lipid bilayer.

The curve at $\hat{T} = 0.8$ reaches much
higher values around the maximum, which can be attributed to the lower thermal excitation.  
The maximum of both curves does not overlap perfectly but is close to $0.85 \si{\nano\m\squared}$
in both cases. This value is also utilized for the self-assembly of the 128 lipid system,
whereas the larger 256 system used an elevated value of $0.87\si{\nano\m\squared}$. 

Comparing the chosen ares per lipid to the typical area per lipid of
a POPC MARTINI bilayer of $0.65 \si{\nano\m\squared}$
shows significant differences. To explain this difference an investigation of the potentials 
of both methods is needed. The area per lipid is greatly influenced by the effective size of 
the beads, which is the distance 
between two beads at which the force is no longer repulsive. For MARTINI and the 
Lennard-Jones potential this is the minimum which depends on the bead types used. 
For the POPC and water system only consisting of regular beads the 
$\sigma$ parameter which governs the effective size reads $\sigma = 0.47\si{\nano\m}$. 
The minimum of the Lennard-Jones potential can be calculated as $1.12 \sigma \approx 0.53\si{\nano\m}$.
As the DPD potential does not have a minimum the force needs to be investigated and 
shows that the effective size of the beads is simply the cutoff at $0.711\si{\nano\m}$.
This deviation in the underlinying forces and potential leads to the
difference in area per lipid for a stable bilayer and is a key difference 
between MARTINI and DPD.

a very similar shape with a rise of the order when elevating the water to lipid ratio 
and becoming more flat at high ratios. This especially holds when taking the largly overlapping

hold the structure. Crucially, when fixing the area per lipid, a change in the water to lipid 
ratio only scales the remaining $z$-axis to maintain the desired density of $\rho = 3$. 
This also explaines the occoring unstabilities at low ratios. The water phase outside the 
bilayer is responsible for shielding the neighboring bilayer from the periodic boundary condition.
For low ratios the water phase is too, leading to interaction between the bilayer leaflets. 
This also explains the flattening of the curve at higher ratios, because if the water phase is 
big enough, further increasing it does not improve stability. To reduce useless simulation of
solvent interaction a low value for the water to lipid ratio which still garantees sufficient 
shielding is optimal. For the self-assembly of 128 lipids this leads to a ratio of 24 and for
256 lipids a ratio of 22. 

To conclude this section
it is cruicial to note that a high nematic order parameter does 
not necessitate a perfectly stable or defect-free bilayer. This means that 
the nematic order parameter is not a perfect parameters to judge the structure of 
a bilayer but is a valid way to get a fast estimate of the bilayer stability. 
Additionally, every simulation run was only performed once and due to the statistical nature of
the DPD force different seed would result in slightly different results. Hence, slight 
differences between measured values should be interpreted with this in mind.

For a quantitative comparison there is a pre-assembled DPD and MARTINI bilayer.
Especially the DPD bilayer serves as a baseline if the self-assembly reached the same 
strcuture as the pre-assembled equilibrated bilayer. The chosen metric for 
the analysis of self-assembly are the neamtic order parameter and the contact number, 
measuring alignment and packing density. The results along snapshots of the 
compared bilayers are shown in \cref{fig:bilayer_128_nem_con}.
The evolution of the nematic order parameter in \cref{fig:128_T_1_nem} matches the observations from 
\cref{fig:bilayer_form_128_T_1}, where the equlibration phase shows no sign of 
assembly and the order is very low in the system. With the cooling phase the order gradually 
rises and plateaus in the production phases. There are fluctuations in the production phase 
but these can mainly be attributed to thermal fluctuations. 

The contact number in \cref{fig:128_T_1_con}
is very similar, apart from the fact that the contact number rises at the bginning meaning that 
even though there is no alignment in the system the lipids start to from clumps even at elevated 
temperatures. The cooling down process results in a rising contact number and the production 
phase again results in a plateau, where fluctuations are again caused by thermal fluctuations.

When compairing both values it is apparent that the self-assembly was successful as both 
the nematic order parameter and the contact number reach the same values the pre-assembled 
DPD bilayer. But the MARTINI bilayer shows significant deviations especially when 
examining the order in the system. The MARTINI bilayer reaches a mean values of about 0.8 whereas
the DPD bilayer has maximum values of only 0.6. This means that the possible alignment in MARTINI 
is much higher than that in a DPD based model. Additionally the contact number also 
reaches smaller values as the MARTINI bilayer, indicating that the packing density 
also lacks in comparison. 

For a more structure based analysis the density profile can be utilized. The results are 
shown in \cref{fig:bilayer_128_density_T_1}.


Looking at both density profiles there are very significant differences in 
the structure of the bilayers. The MARTINI bilayer clearly seperates each bead group. The 
peaks do not overlap there are regions of the bilayer where a specific group is not 
located. This is perfectly illustrated by the fact that the curve for the heads is 
constantly zero in the terminal tail regime. The same behavior can not be 
observed for the DPD bilayer. There, especially the head and first tail groups overlap greatly.
Furthermore, there are still some heads in the terminal tail regime. 
The combined plot \cref{fig:128_high_T_combined_den} illustrates this well and 
shows that every peak in DPD in wider and penetrates in the other regimes. 
Especially the head beads still contribute to the density when there should be an 
apolar phase. One explanation for this is that DPD simply allows the polar heads to pass
through beads to get inside the bilayer which can also sometimes be seen in some  
snapshots like \cref{fig:128_high_T_selfbilayer}. Additionally this can be 
exhilarated by a membrane that is not perfectly planar like the martini bilayer in \cref{fig:martini_comp}.
Another big differnece which can be read from \cref{fig:128_high_T_combined_den} and confirmed by 
the comparison of the snapshots is the reduced thickness of the DPD bilayer.

\section{Bilayer formation of larger systems}

Before showing the results for the larger sytsem of 256 lipids it is important to emphasize 
that the self-assembly process becomes much more difficult for larger systems. This 
means that cylindrical structures, vesicles or even grid like formations begin to 
form in favor of flat bilayers. This was also the motivation behind the finetuning of the area
per lipid and the water to lipid ratio. Additionally, as \cref{sec:larger_systems} 
mentioned this led to a protocol which rescaled box dimensions to allow for bilayer formation. 

The analysis steps are the same as in \cref{sec:self_ass_128} and are shown in \cref{fig:256_high_T_all_info}.
The formation process look very similar to the smaller bilayers with no strcutres as $\hat{T} = 2.0$,
clumping during the cooling process and then stabilization at the target temperatreu of $\hat{T} = 1.0$.
The order parameter reaches the same values as the smaller counterpart but in the contact number 
at the beginning of the production phase, there is a strong dip. This can 
attributed to the box rescaling that was discussed above where for a small time each lipid had 
more space which means the distance between them will increase lowering the contact number. 
There is still a significant discrepancy to the MARTINI system. The quality of the bilayer
can be observed in \cref{fig:256_mol_lipidsoup}, \cref{fig:256_mol_transition} and 
\cref{fig:256_mol_bilayer} matches the evolution of the samller systems with a 
lipid soup clumping together while cooling down the system and resulting in a bilayer.
