\chapter*{Abstract}

This work utilizes a coarse-grained DPD force field based on the MARTINI method, proposed by Wang and Hernandez (\textit{ACS Omega, 2024}), to investigate membrane self-assembly and the reproducibility of membrane properties. The evaluation assesses packing density, alignment, and phase separation. Furthermore, the study computes the area per lipid, membrane thickness, and bending modulus, comparing these results to their MARTINI counterparts. A power spectrum analysis, following the methodology of Cooke and Deserno (\textit{J. Chem. Phys. 2005}), determines the bending modulus. Successful self-assembly processes yield a structure that closely resembles a MARTINI bilayer. However, the increased effective size of DPD beads results in an elevated area per lipid of approximately $0.85\si{\nano\m\squared}$ compared to the MARTINI value of $0.65\si{\nano\m\squared}$. Additionally, the soft DPD interaction reduces the bilayer thickness to $2.9\si{\nano\m}$, contrasted with $4.1\si{\nano\m}$ for MARTINI. Similarly, the bending modulus decreases significantly to $5.2k_\text{B}T$ compared to the MARTINI value of $19.0k_\text{B}T$. The softness of the DPD force permits substantial overlap between beads, which explains these deviations.

\chapter*{Zusammenfassung}

Diese Arbeit nutzt ein grobkörniges (coarse-grained) DPD-Kraftfeld basierend auf der MARTINI-Methode, vorgeschlagen von Wang und Hernandez (\textit{ACS Omega, 2024}), um die Membran-Selbstassemblierung und die Reproduzierbarkeit von Membraneigenschaften zu untersuchen. Die Auswertung beinhaltet Analyse der Packungsdichte, der Ausrichtung (alignment) und die Phasenseparation. Darüber hinaus wird in dieser Arbeit der Platzbedarf pro Lipid (area per lipid), die Membrandicke sowie das Biegemodul (bending modulus) berechnet und vergleichen mit den entsprechenden MARTINI Ergebnissen. Eine Power-Spektrum-Analyse, welche der Methodik von Cooke und Deserno (\textit{J. Chem. Phys. 2005}) folgt, bestimmt das Biegemodul. Erfolgreiche Selbstassemblierungsprozesse liefern eine Struktur, die einem MARTINI-Bilayer stark ähnelt. Jedoch führt die erhöhte effektive Größe der DPD-Beads zu einem gesteigerten Platzbedarf pro Lipid von etwa $0.85\si{\nano\m\squared}$ im Vergleich zum MARTINI-Wert von $0.65\si{\nano\m\squared}$. Zusätzlich reduziert die weiche DPD-Wechselwirkung die Bilayer-Dicke auf $2.9\si{\nano\m}$, im Kontrast zu $4.1\si{\nano\m}$ bei MARTINI. Ebenso sinkt der Biegemodul signifikant auf $5.2k_\text{B}T$ verglichen mit dem MARTINI-Wert von $19.0k_\text{B}T$. Die Weichheit der DPD-Kraft ermöglicht eine erhebliche Überlappung zwischen den Beads, was diese Abweichungen erklärt.

\chapter{Introduction}

Computer simulations provide an important technique to investigate biophysical processes that remain difficult to access experimentally. While all-atom force fields simulate every interaction individually and deliver very accurate results, they require substantial computational power. Coarse-grained models overcome this problem by reducing the degrees of freedom.

This work proposes a coarse-grained implementation of Dissipative Particle Dynamics (DPD), first introduced by Groot and Warren \cite{DPD_groot_warren}, utilizing the MARTINI force field as a baseline for parametrization \cite{Wang2024}. DPD offers advantages over other force fields due to its integrated thermostat and soft-core interactions, which facilitate faster dynamics. The study tests the implemented DPD model on lipid membrane self-assembly across different system sizes and temperatures. The investigation focuses on homogeneous membranes consisting of 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC), a lipid that plays a significant role in biophysics. Choosing the self-assembly process provides a robust benchmark to evaluate the capabilities of the implementation. The evaluation assesses the membrane based on packing density and general alignment. Furthermore, the analysis examines multiple membrane properties, specifically the area per lipid, membrane thickness, and the bending modulus. The latter is computed by implementing a power spectrum analysis \cite{Cooke_bending_mod}.

First, a comprehensive theory section introduces important concepts and the foundations for the implementation and evaluation. Next, the methods section explores the transition from MARTINI to a coarse-grained DPD implementation, including a detailed description of the lipid system topology, the conversion into DPD units, and the simulation protocols. Lastly, a discussion of the results follows, with a strong focus on comparing DPD and MARTINI.



\chapter{Theory}

\section{Coarse-Grained Simulations}
Coarse-grained simulations \cite{CGwater_review} aim to simulate the behavior of large systems by reducing the 
available degrees of freedom in the system, while preserving the main thermodynamic and
structural properties. The primary technique is a mapping mechanism that
represents groups of atoms as a single interaction site or “bead”. The number of atoms 
that are coarse-grained differ between different methods and the length scale of interest.

Coarse-graining methods provide four major advantages that improve performance \cite{cg_review_speedups}. A reduction in the degrees of freedom by the mapping mechanism, decreases the number of particles to compute. Furthermore, omitting atomistic detail smooths the potential energy surface and lowers friction and leads to faster dynamics. Typically, coarse-grained methods employ a truncated interaction force and a larger timestep. While all atom simulations utilize a timestep on the order of femtoseconds\cite{Atomic_timescales}, coarse-graining allows these to be extended into tens or hundreds of femtoseconds, depending on the method.  

A central challenge in CG simulations is parametrization. Two main 
strategies exist: utilizing experimental data
or the results from more accurate all atom simulations to define the CG interactions.
Since not every thermodynamic property can be captured simultaneously, 
models are chosen to reproduce the desired properties \cite{CGwater_review}.

This work employs the mapping method of the MARTINI force field \cite{Martini}, the details of which are explained in 
\cref{martini}. 


\section{The MARTINI Model}\label{martini}

The MARTINI force field \cite{Martini} is one of the most widely used methods for coarse-grained simulations.
Central to this approach is the representation of different groups of atoms, 
MARTINI distinguishes between five basic bead 
types: water (W), polar (P), non-polar (N), apolar (C), charged (Q).
The P and C beads are also assigned a number from 1 to 5 depending on the 
polarity, reaching from P1 being the least polar and P5 being the most
polar whereas C1 is the most apolar and C5 the least apolar.
The W bead is identical to the P4 bead. The Q and N beads can also have 
subtypes which quantify the hydrogen bond forming capabilities.
There is 0 meaning no capabilities, d meaning some donor capabilities and 
a meaning some acceptor capabilities. The coarse-graining degree is 4:1 for most structures, while smaller structures like rings utilize 3:1 or a 2:1 mapping.    

While the MARTINI scheme usually utilizes the Lennard-Jones potential and the corresponding
force for non-bonded beads, using the underlying interaction matrix $\epsilon_{ij}$ to determine the interaction.This work employs another force field, explained in \cref{dpd}
while adapting the same 
mapping mechanism of the MARTINI method. 

\section{Dissipative Particle Dynamics} \label{dpd}
The DPD force field was introduced by Groot and Warren for the simulation of systems at 
the mesoscopic scale \cite{DPD_groot_warren} and is widely used for fluid simulations.
The equations of motion for a particle $i$ interacting with surrounding particles $j$
in a DPD force field are governed by

\begin{equation}
    m \frac{\text{d}\mathbf{v}_i}{\text{d}t} = \sum_{j \neq i} \mathbf{F}^C_{ij} + 
    \mathbf{F}^D_{ij} + \mathbf{F}^R_{ij}\, ,  
\end{equation}

consisting of three different components: the conservative
force $\mathbf{F}^C$, the dissipative force $\mathbf{F}^D$ and the random force $\mathbf{F}^R$. 

The conservative force is defined as

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{F}^C_{ij} = \begin{cases}
        A_{ij}\left (1 - \frac{r_{ij}}{r_c} \right )\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}, & r_{ij} \leq r_c \\
        0, & r_{ij} > r_c 
    \end{cases}
    \, ,
\end{equation}

where $r_c$ represents the cutoff distance and $A_{ij}$ is the maximum repulsion between 
a specific particle pair. Furthermore, the relative position is given by the distance vector
$\mathbf{r}_{ij} = \mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j$, with $r_{ij} = |\mathbf{r}_{ij}|$ as its magnitude and $\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij} = \mathbf{r}_{ij}/r_{ij}$ the corresponding unit vector.

Typically, the cutoff distance is set to $r_c = 1$ when working with reduced units, which will be 
discussed in \cref{dpd_units}, 
which simplifies the expression above. Both force fields are compared in \cref{comp_forces} 
to further illustrate the difference between the MARTINI method 
in \cref{martini}, based on the Lennard-Jones potential, parametrized by the length scale $\sigma$ and the
energy scale $\epsilon$
\begin{equation}
    U^{LJ} = 4 \epsilon \left [\left(\frac{\sigma}{r} \right)^{12} 
    - \left(\frac{\sigma}{r} \right)^6 \right ]\,  \label{LJ_pot}
\end{equation}

and the previously defined DPD force.
The key distinciton between the two
approaches is that the DPD force and the corresponding potential
do not diverge at $r=0$, which allows for particle overlap and characterizes it as a soft potential. Additionally, DPD is a purely repulsive 
force, whereas Lennard-Jones includes an attractive component and a potential minimum.
Furthermore, the DPD force is a short-range interaction, while the Lennard-Jones force  theoretically has an infinite range but is typically truncated to improve computational efficiency.

The dissipative and random force can best be described by using two weight 
functions $w^D$ and $w^R$. Using the work of Espanol and Warren \cite{Espanol_warren}
one weight function can be chosen arbitrarily, which in turn determines the other one. 
The common choice for the weighting function is

\begin{equation}
    w^D(r_{ij}) = \left [w^R(r_{ij}) \right ]^2 = \begin{cases}
        \left (1 - \frac{r_{ij}}{r_c} \right )^2, & r_{ij} \leq r_c \\
        0, & r_{ij} > r_c
    \end{cases}\, .
\end{equation}

The magnitude of both forces are governed by scaling coefficients: $\sigma$ for the random force
and $\gamma$ for the dissipative force. The fluctuation dissipation theorem connects these
two parameters with the thermal Energy $k_B T$ and yields

\begin{equation}
    \sigma^2 = 2 \gamma k_B T\, . \label{connection_fluc_dis}
\end{equation}

By defining $\mathbf{v}_{ij} = \mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{v}_j$ as the
relative velocity between two particles
and $\alpha_{ij}$ as 
a stochastic variable with zero mean and unit variance, 
the final expression for both forces is obtained

\begin{align}
    \mathbf{F}^D_{ij} &= -\gamma w^D(r_{ij})(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}\cdot \mathbf{v}_{ij})
    \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij} \\
    \mathbf{F}^R_{ij} &= \sigma w^R(r_{ij}) \alpha_{ij} \Delta t^{-1/2} \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}\, .
\end{align}

Notably, a factor of $\Delta t^{-1/2}$ appears in the random force expression. This correction
follows the work of Groot and Warren \cite{DPD_groot_warren} and
ensures that the mean square displacement follows the correct time dependency
$\left < x^2\right >\propto t$.

As previously mentioned, both forces are linked by the fluctuation dissipation theorem 
\cref{connection_fluc_dis}. This assures that the energy added by the random force
$\mathbf{F}^R$ 
is exactly balanced by the energy dissipated through force $\mathbf{F}^D$. Consequently, both forces act as an internal 
thermostat, allowing sampling of the canonical ensemble and eliminating the need for any
external thermostats during simulation.

To fully describe DPD interaction, the only parameters to determine are the 
repulsion parameters $A_{ij}$ and the drag coefficient $\gamma$ which in turn fixes the 
random force parameter $\sigma$. This step will be further discussed in \cref{sec:dpd_parameters}.

\section{Electrostatic Interaction}\label{coulomb}

The standard DPD force in \cref{dpd} does not differentiate between a charged or an uncharged bead. Therefore, 
an electrostatic interaction has must be integrated into the force field, 
or the repulsion have to be adjusted accordingly. Crucially, a simple 
coulomb potential is unsuitable, as its divergence at $r=0$ would negate the softness of the DPD potential. To address this, 
an exponentially smeared charge distribution, resulting in a Slater potential, is employed 
. This follows the work of Wang and Hernandez \cite{Wang2024}
who introduce a factor $\beta$ which controls the extend of the
smearing effect. The charge distribution then reads

\begin{equation}
    \rho(r) = \frac{q\beta^2}{\pi r} \exp(-2\beta r)\, . \label{charge_density}
\end{equation}

Utilizing a conversion constant $C$, the resulting electrostatic force between two beads $i$ and $j$ follows

\begin{align}
    \mathbf{F}^E_{ij} = \frac{C q_i q_j}{4 \pi r_{ij}}[1 - \exp(-2\beta r_{ij})
    (1+2\beta r_{ij}(1+\beta r_{ij}))]\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}\, . \label{coulomb_force}
\end{align}


\section{Intramolecular Interactions}\label{intermol}

Beyond the non-bonded interactions defined in \cref{dpd} and \cref{coulomb}, intramolecular potentials
involving various numbers of participating beads ensure the structural integrity of the molecules \cite{Martini}.
Two-body interactions are defined as bonds and determine the equilibrium distance
between two beads,
while angle potentials control the local geometry between three consecutive beads. 
For four-body interactions
a dihedral potential is utilized to characterize the torsion around a central bond, preserving 
properties such as the orientation of specific atomic groups. Lastly, improper dihedrals 
maintain the chirality or planarity of molecular groups.

The specific potentials depend on the molecule being represented, and not every molecule utilizes every
type of interaction. A more detailed application of these bonded will be discussed in \cref{topology}.

\section{Numerical Integration}

The forces defined in \cref{dpd}, \cref{coulomb} and \cref{intermol} result in a system of
ordinary differential equations that must be solved in molecular dynamics simulations. Therefore, 
numerical integrators are employed that partition time into discrete time steps of size $\Delta t$. The standard algorithm in LAMMPS is the Velocity Verlet integrator \cite{Allen2017_verlet} which
computes the poistion and velocity of a bead at $t+ \Delta t$, based on the state of the previous timestep 
$t$. The iterative procedure is

\begin{alignat}{2}
    &1.\ \text{Predict half step velocity} \quad &&\mathbf{v}(t + \Delta t/2) = \mathbf{v}(t) + 
    \mathbf{a}(t) \Delta t/2\\
    &2.\ \text{Update positions} \quad&&\mathbf{x}(t + \Delta t) = \mathbf{x}(t) + 
    \mathbf{v}(t + \Delta t/2)\Delta t\\
    &3.\ \text{Calculate new acceleration} \quad&&\mathbf{a}(t + \Delta t) = 
    \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}(t + \Delta t), \mathbf{v}(t + \Delta t/2))/m\\
    &4.\ \text{Correct final velocity} \quad&&\mathbf{v}(t + \Delta t) = 
    \mathbf{v}(t + \Delta t/2) + \mathbf{a}(t + \Delta t)\Delta t/2\, .
\end{alignat}

This scheme is fitting for DPD as the dissipative force is velocity-dependant. Utilizing the intermediate velocity at $t+\Delta t/2$ allows for a better treatment of the forces. Furthermore, this scheme \cite{Allen2017_verlet} is numerically stable 
and conserves energy even for large timesteps. 


\section{Periodic Boundary Condition}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=14cm]{figures/theory/pbc.png}
    \caption{Visualization of the periodic boundary condition in LAMMPS. The left shows a single

    system after employing the periodic boundary condition with interactions beyond the single cell boundaries.}
    \label{fig:pbc}

Computer simulation face the challenge of being limited to finite systems. This 
limitation leads to unphysical surface effects when particles reach the boundaries of the system. 
Numerous possibilities exist to mitigate this
but the standard mechanism is to enforce a periodic boundary condition (PBC) \cite{LammpsBoundary}. Employing a 
PBC means that a particle leaving the simulation box simultaneously re-enters from the opposing boundary. Furthermore, 
a particle near a boundary also interacts with the particles on the other side of the box via the nearest
image convention, meaning that any particle only interacts with the closest periodic image of its neighbors. 
Effectively, this creates
a system that behaves like an infinite grid of the same finite system. The effect of the PBC is 
visualized in \cref{fig:pbc}.

\section{Assembly parameters}\label{nematic}

The degree of order in a system is a critical metric when simulating self-assembly, as 
it quantifies the macroscopic state of the system. The nematic order parameter 
\cite{Ramasubramani2020_freud}, \cite{FreudNematicDoc} measures 
the collective alignment of particles. The parameter is derived from the traceless second-order
tensor $\mathbf{Q}$, defined as 

\begin{align}
    \mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{M} - \frac{1}{3}\mathbf{I}\, ,
\end{align}

where $\mathbf{M}$ is the orientation tensor and $\mathbf{I}$ is the identity matrix.
The components of $\mathbf{M}$ are calculated using the normalized orientation vector $\mathbf{m}^{(i)}$ 
of the particle $i \in \{1,\dots,N \}$ and reads

\begin{align}
    \mathbf{M}_{\alpha \beta} = \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{m}_\alpha^{(i)}\mathbf{m}_\beta^{(i)}\, .
\end{align}

The eigenvalues of $\mathbf{Q}$ are $\frac{2}{3}S$, $-\frac{1}{3}S$ and $-\frac{1}{3}S$, where $S$ represents
the desired order parameter. The eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue is called the 
director, indicating the principal alignment of the system. 

In addition to alignment, the packing density is quantified to ensure that the lipids form a connected structure. For this purpose, a 
contact number is defined as the average number of neighboring lipids within a specific cutoff distance $r_c$. This metric is highly dependent on this cutoff parameters, which is determined in \cref{sec:128_assembly}.

\section{Membrane Fluctuations} \label{sec:ben_mod_fourier}

The general energy of a membrane \cite{Markus_tethers} is defined as an integral over the 
membrane area, governed by two moduli: $\kappa$, the bending modulus and $\bar{\kappa}$,
the saddle splay modulus 

\begin{align}
    E = \int \mathrm d A \left[ \frac{1}{2}\kappa 
    (K - C_0)^2 + \bar{\kappa}K_G \right]\, , \label{eq:helfrich_energy}
\end{align}

where $K$ is the mean curvature, the Gaussian curvature $K_G$ and the intrinsic curvature $C_0$.
Utilizing the Monge parametrization with a height function $h(x,y)$, the mean curvature \cite{Markus_tethers}
of an almost flat membrane can be approximated as

\begin{align}
    K = \nabla \cdot \left( \frac{\nabla h}{\sqrt{1 + (\nabla h)^2}}\right)
    \approx \Delta h \, .
\end{align}

Including the lateral tension $\Sigma$, this results in the following energy expression \cite{Cooke_bending_mod}

\begin{align}
    E = \frac{1}{2} \int \mathrm d\mathbf x \left[ 
        \kappa (\Delta h(\mathbf x))^2 + \Sigma (\nabla h(\mathbf x))^2 \right]\, . \label{eq:mem_energy}
\end{align}

To further investigate
the membrane fluctuations \cite{Schwarz2024},
the height function is expressed 
via its inverse Fourier transform 

\begin{align}
    h(\mathbf x) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)} \int \mathrm d\mathbf k 
    \tilde{h}(\mathbf k) \exp(i\mathbf k \cdot \mathbf x)\, , 
\end{align}

with the wave vector $\mathbf k$. It is instructive to introduce 
the dirac delta distribution represented in Fourier space 

\begin{align}
    (2\pi)^2 \delta(\mathbf k) = \int \mathrm d \mathbf x \exp(i\mathbf k \cdot \mathbf x)\, .
\end{align}

The height function is purely real. Consequently, $h(\mathbf x) = h(\mathbf x)^*$, leading to 
the relation in Fourier space $h(\mathbf k)^* = h(-\mathbf k)$. Inserting the results into 
\cref{eq:mem_energy} yields 

\begin{align}
    E = \frac{1}{2} \int \mathrm d \mathbf k 
    |\tilde{h}(\mathbf k)|^2 \left[\kappa k^4 + \Sigma k^2 \right]\, .
\end{align}

Applying the equipartition theorem, which relates the average of each squared mode  $\langle |\tilde{h}
(\mathbf k)|^2\rangle$ to the thermal energy of $k_{\mathrm B} T/2 $, leads to the 
expression 

\begin{align}
    \langle |\tilde{h}(\mathbf k)|^2\rangle = \frac{k_{\mathrm B}T}{\kappa k^4 + \Sigma k^2}\, .
\end{align}

For practical application, a discrete Fourier transform is employed 

\begin{align}
    h(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)}\sum_\mathbf{k} \tilde{h}_\mathbf{k} \exp(i \mathbf k \cdot \mathbf x) 
\end{align}

with discrete wave vectors $\mathbf k = 2\pi/L\cdot (n_x,n_y)$.
For a membrane of the area $A = L^2$ this discrete approach leads to the final expression for the fluctuation power spectrum

\begin{align}
    \langle |\tilde{h}_\mathbf{k}|^2\rangle = \frac{k_{\mathrm B}T}{L^2(\kappa k^4 + \Sigma k^2)}\, . \label{eq:cental_ben_formula_fourier}
\end{align}

Notably, an additional factor of $1/L^2$ arises from the transition to a discrete basis. In this context, the continuous Dirac delta distribution $\delta(\mathbf{k})$ is replaced by its discrete counterpart $L^2 \delta_{\mathbf{k}, -\mathbf{k}}$. Dimensionality analysis confirms this substitution, as $[\delta(\mathbf{k})]$ and $[L^2 \delta_{\mathbf{k}, -\mathbf{k}}]$ possess the dimensions of area.

\section{System Topology}\label{topology}

The molecular topology is defined in the \path{martini_v_2.0_POPC.itp} file, 
obtained from the MARTINI website \cite{Martini,MartiniLipidome}. It serves as the baseline for the 
parametrization in the following sections. It lists the employed potential for the 
bonds and angles, including their equilibrium parameters in MARTINI units, as well
as the bead types used in the representation.

\cref{martini} introduces the basic bead types in the MARTINI method. In this work the system consists 
of POPC lipids and water molecules acting as the solvent. Each water bead is a P4 bead and 
contains four classical water molecules. 
The POPC lipids consist of a choline head group (Q0, bead 1), a phosphate group (Qa, bead 2) and two 
glycerol groups (Na, beads 3 and 4) that connect the polar head to two hydrophobic tails. These tails are comprised of an oleoyl (C1, beads 5, 6, 8; C3, bead 7)
and a palmitoyl group (C1, beads 9, 10, 11, 12) and are highly apolar. Each bead is assigned a unique ID ranging from
one to twelve to precisely reference specific bead. The resulting coarse-grained molecule, consisting of 
five unique bead types and twelve total beads
is illustrated in \cref{fig:popc_lammps}. The relative bead positions are based on MARTINI mapping and were extracted from the 
\path{POPC-em.gro} file \cite{Martini,MartiniLipidome}. The beads are connected by eleven bonds and eight angles, categorized into 
two unique bond types and three angle types, as detailed in \cref{tab:bond_angle_connections}.
There are no dihedral or improper interactions in the POPC molecule.

While \cref{intermol} defined the general interaction between beads inside a molecule, the specific bond potential is implemented as a simple harmonic potential
in LAMMPS \cite{LammpsBond} 

\begin{equation}
    E_{ij} = \hat{K}^b(r_{ij} - r^0_{ij})^2. \label{harmonic}\, ,
\end{equation}

where $K_b$ represents the bond stiffness, containing the usual 
factor of $1/2$, and $r^0_{ij}$ is the equilibrium
bond distance of the bead pair.
Similarly, the angle potential \cite{LammpsAngle} is a cosine-squared function

\begin{equation}
    E_{ijk} = \hat{K}^a[\cos(\theta_{ijk} - \cos(\theta^0_{ijk}))]^2\, , \label{cosine/squared}
\end{equation}


where $K_a$ serves as the angular stiffness and  $\theta^0_{ijk}$ denotes the equilibrium angle between the three beads. Although this choice 
is dictated by the MARTINI force field, it is worth noting why a cosine based potential is preferred over a simple harmonic one. The formulation in \cref{cosine/squared}
is significantly more computationally efficient, as it avoids the use of the $\arccos(\theta)$ function, which is expensive and numerically sensitive.
A detailed computation of the equilibrium parameters is done in \cref{sec:dpd_bond_params}.

\section{DPD Units}\label{dpd_units}

Molecular dynamics simulations frequently employ reduced units,
which utilize reference parameters to render physical quantities
dimensionless. For MARTINI these are typically the Lennard-Jones parameters 
$\epsilon$ and $\sigma$, defined in \cref{LJ_pot}. However, since DPD utilizes a different 
force and potential, an alternative set of reference parameters is required. Following the work of Wang and Hernandez \cite{Wang2024}, the length scale is defined based on the volume required to contain three CG water beads. This 
choice is not arbitrary as it 
follows the early
work of Groot and Warren 
\cite{DPD_groot_warren} who established a standard number density of $\rho=3$ DPD beads per unit volume $V = r_{\text{ref}}^3$. 
In the MARTINI model, every water bead represents $N_w = 4$ classical molecules. 
The volume of a single water molecule, $V_w$, can be calculated using its
molar mass $M=18.02 \si{\g\per\mol}$,
the density $\rho_w = 1 \si{\g\per\cubic\centi\meter}$ and the Avogadro
constant $N_A = 6.022 \cdot 10^{23} 
\text{molecules}/\text{mol}$

\begin{equation}
    V_w = \frac{M}{\rho_w N_A} = 2.99 \cdot 10^{-23} \si{\cubic\centi\m} 
    = 29.9 \si{\cubic\angstrom}\, .
\end{equation}

The DPD reference length follows directly from the previous computations

\begin{equation}
    r_{\text{ref}} = \sqrt[3]{\rho \cdot N_w \cdot V_w} = 7.11 \si{\angstrom}\, ,
\end{equation}

which serves as the basis to convert any physical length into DPD units through normalization by $r_\text{ref}$.

The energy scale is defined more directly. By choosing the reference temperature as
$T_{\text{ref}}=298.15 \si{\K}$, the thermal energy at this temperature is used as reference unit 

\begin{equation}
    \epsilon_{\text{ref}} = k_{\mathrm{B}} T_{\text{ref}} = 4.12 \cdot 10^{-21} \si{\J} = 
    25.7 \si{\milli\eV}\, .
\end{equation}

Similar to the length scale, this energy scale allows for the conversion of every energy value to 
be converted into DPD units by division. Furthermore, the combination of these two scales can be 
employed to render physical quantities dimensionless. 

Since this work aims to investigate dynamic processes, specifically the self-assembly, it is crucial
to establish a timescale. While this usually entails a simple combination of the 
existing scales, the DPD timescale has to be handled with more care. 
Following the work of Wang and Hernandez \cite{Wang2024} the timescale is calculated by 
matching the diffusion constant of DPD water to its experimental value. This leads to 
a timescale of 

\begin{align}
    \tau = 91.4\si{\pico\s}\, .
\end{align}


\section{Determination of DPD Bonded Parameters}\label{sec:dpd_bond_params}

The \path{martini_v_2.0_POPC.itp} file provides the equilibrium parameters, as detailed in \cref{topology}. The MARTINI database \cite{Martini,MartiniLipidome} specifies these parameters in SI units, specifically, it uses 
$[r] = \si{\nano\m}$ for distances and $[k^b] = \si{\kilo\J\per\mol\per\nano\m\squared}$ for the bond stiffness.
Similarly, the database defines the angle stiffness $[k^a] = \si{\kilo\J\per\mol}$, reflecting that angles are intrinsically dimensionless.

The conversion into DPD units employs the reference scales defined in \cref{dpd_units}
Besides the application of the scales, the MARTINI units are divided by the avogadro constant to account for their molar basis.
The bond spring 
constant in DPD unit, $\hat{K}^b$, follows as 

\begin{align}
    \hat{K}^b = \frac{1}{2} k^b \frac{r_{\text{ref}}^2}{e_{\text{ref}}N_A}\, , \label{eq:bond_spring_conversion}
\end{align}

where $k^b$ is the equilibrium force constant from MARTINI with the included factor of $1/2$ discussed in \cref{topology}. 
The conversion of the equilibrium distances requires a simple scaling

\begin{align}
    \hat{r}^0 = r^0/r_{\text{ref}}\, .
\end{align}

The angle spring constant follows a pattern identical to \cref{eq:bond_spring_conversion}, 
yielding

\begin{align}
    \hat{K}^a = \frac{1}{2}  \frac{k^a}{e_{\text{ref}}N_A}\, ,
\end{align}

where $k^a$ denotes the original MARTINI value. The POPC molecule utilizes multiple bond and angle types, as specified
in \cref{topology}. \cref{tab:bond_angle_params} summarizes the converted values alongside their MARTINI counterparts for each type. The simulations employ these finalized parameters.

\section{Determination of DPD Charge}

Following the introduction in \cref{coulomb}, a slater potential models the coulombic interaction, as detailed in \cref{charge_density} and \cref{coulomb_force}. The model utilizes a strength parameter 
derived from earlier works from Gonzales-Melchor et al \cite{Gonzales-Melchor_beta}, who 
determine a value of $\beta_3 = 0.929$ for a coarse-graining degree of $N = 3$.
Wang and Hernandez \cite{Wang2024} scaled this factor $\beta$ 
to the applied coarse-graining of $N = 4$ under the assumption of linearity. 
Because the LAMMPS implementation \cite{LammpsSlater} requires the inverse value $\lambda = 1/\beta$, the conversion yields  

    
\begin{align}
    \beta = 0.844 = 1/\lambda = 1/1.185\, .
\end{align}
    
The introduced length and energy scales also impact the charge scaling. To calibrate 
the charge, the potential energy of two point charges in SI units is equated
with the corresponding energy in DPD units \cite{Wang2024,Allen2017_verlet}, 
resulting in

\begin{align}
    &\frac{q^2}{4\pi \epsilon_0 r}  = 
    \frac{\hat{q}^2}{\hat{r}} e_{\text{ref}}\\
    &\hat{q} = \frac{q}{\sqrt{4\pi \epsilon_0 r_{\text{ref}}e_{\text{ref}}}} = 8.86\, .
\end{align}

Under this definition, one elemental charge in SI units maps 
to a dimensionless DPD charge of $\hat{q} = 8.86$. Standard LAMMPS convention absorbs the factors of $4\pi\epsilon_0$ directly into the charge definition.

\section{Determination of DPD Force Parameters}\label{sec:dpd_parameters}

The repulsion parameters $A_{ij}$ constitute the core of the DPD force field,
as they determine the maximum repulsion of the conservative force introduced in \cref{dpd}.
In addition, the model requires the definition of the drag parameter $\gamma$.
The work of Groot and Warren \cite{DPD_groot_warren} provides the basis for this value, identifying $\hat{\sigma} = 3$ as the optimal value for the random force parameter at a reduced temperature of $\hat{T} = 1$.
Applying the fluctuation dissipation theorem and 
setting the thermal energy $k_{\text{b}}T$ to unity yields
 
\begin{align}
    \hat{\gamma} = 4.5\, .
\end{align}

Within LAMMPS, the drag parameter $\hat{\gamma}$ serves as a fixed value in the force field implementation. Changing the target temperature of the DPD force automatically scales
the random force parameter $\hat{\sigma}$, according to the fluctuation dissipation theorem.

Wang and Hernandez \cite{Wang2024} derived the repulsion parameters used in this simulation. 
\cref{tab:dpd_params} presents these values, while the following section details derivation method.

The repulsion between two water beads $A_{ww}$ serves as the baseline. 
Determining this value involves matching the inverse compressibility $\kappa^{-1}$ from experimental data 
to DPD simulation data. This approach yields 

\begin{align}
    A_{ww} = 102.067\, .
\end{align}


at a number density of $\rho = 3$.
When correcting for coarse-graining, this value agrees well with the value found by Groot at Warren \cite{DPD_groot_warren} of $a = 25$, as the factor 4 is simply the coarse-graining degree. 

The MARTINI model applies the effective Lennard-Jones radius
$\sigma_{ww}$ from water-water interaction to all identical bead pairs, enforcing the condition 
$\sigma_{AA} = \sigma_{ww}$. Analogously, this work assigns the determined repulsion parameter $A_{ww}$to every interaction between identical beads, resulting in $A_{AA} = A_{ww}$ for all bead types A.

The following expression defines the interaction between two distinc beads $(A \neq B)$ as

\begin{equation}
    A_{AB} = A_{ww} + \Delta A_{AB}\, .
\end{equation}

The parameter $\Delta A_{AB}$ is related to the Flory-Huggins parameter 

\begin{equation}
    \chi_{AB} = \lambda_\chi \Delta A_{AB}\, 
\end{equation}

where the fitting parameter $\lambda_\chi$ links the two models.
Earlier work by Groot and Rabone \cite{GROOT_xi_scale} demonstrate that the Flory-Huggins 
parameter $\chi_{AB}$ scales linearly with the coarse-graining degree, $N_m = 4$
molecules per bead, yielding 

\begin{equation}
    \chi_{AB} = \bar{\chi}_{AB} N_m\, .
\end{equation}

The final expression for the repulsion parameter reads

\begin{equation}
    A_{AB} = A_{ww} + \bar{\chi}_{AB} N_m/\lambda_\chi\, . \label{final_form_dpd}
\end{equation}

Determining the fitting parameter $\lambda_\chi$ requires a phase seperation simulation within a box initially containing two different bead types $A$ and $B$.
The simulation measures the equilibrium volume fraction $\phi$. Repeating this procedure for 
different values of $\Delta a_{AB}$ provides the necessary data point for the calculation. For each run, the measured volume fraction determines the Flory-Huggins parameter $\chi$ according to

\begin{equation}
    \chi = \frac{\ln[(1-\phi)/\phi]}{1 - 2\phi}\, .
\end{equation}

A linear fit of the resulting data, plotted against the parameter $\Delta A_{AB}$, identifies the fitting parameter from its slope 

\begin{align}
    \lambda_\chi = 0.28\, .
\end{align}

Wang and Hernandez \cite{Wang2024} utilize a baseline interaction matrix 
$\epsilon_{AB}$ to calculate the size-dependant Flory-Hugigns parameter.

\begin{equation}
    \bar{\chi}_{AB}N_m = \chi_{AB} = \frac{z}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T} \left[\epsilon_{AB} - 
    \frac{1}{2} (\epsilon_{AA} + \epsilon_{BB}) \right] \, ,
\end{equation}

where $z$ represents the effective number of nearest neighbors.
The MARTINI model \cite{Martini} serves as the baseline interaction matrix. Substituting the resulting values into \cref{final_form_dpd} yields the final repulsion coefficient. 
This work employs the parameters displayed in \cref{tab:dpd_params} for every simulation.

\section{System Setup}\label{sec:sys_setup}

Initiating a simulation in LAMMPS requires an input files. A header specifies
the number of beads, the count of bond, angle, dihedral, improper types (\cref{intermol}),
the box dimensions and the bead masses. Following the header, specific sections define the position of every beads (atoms sections), the connections by bonds (bons section), the connections by angles (angles sections). The file concludes with the equilibrium parameters for each bond and angle type.
Although dihedral and improper interactions typically require dedicated sections, this work omits them as previously discussed.

A Python script generates this file using several inputs: the number of lipids $N_l$, the number density $\rho$, the area per lipid $A_l$, and the water-to-lipid ratio $R_{w/l} = M_w/N_l$, where $N_w$ denotes the quantity of water beads. 

\begin{align}
    A_l = \frac{L_x L_y}{N_l/2}\, , \label{eq:def_apl}
\end{align}

defines the area per lipid, assuming a square bilayer in the $xy$-plane with dimensions $L_x$ and $L_y$. The number density 
is a set parameter at $\rho=3$. This not only follows the work of Groot and Warren \cite{DPD_groot_warren} but is also mandatory as Wang and Hernandez \cite{Wang2024} determined the repulsion parameters at $\rho = 3$.
The following sections examine the other two inputs, the area per lipid $A_l$ and the water-to-lipid ratio $R_{w/l}$ in greater detail.

\cref{topology} and \cref{tab:bond_angle_connections} provide the specific counts of for each bead, bond, angle, improper and dihedral type. For the POPC-water system, this configuration yields six bead types, two bond types and three angle types.

The script computes the total quantities of beads, beads, bonds and angles using the per-molecule definitions from \cref{tab:bond_angle_connections} and the input parameters $N_l$ and $R_{w/l}$

\begin{align}
    &N(\text{beads}) = (12 + R_{w/l}) \cdot N_l \label{eq:num_beads}\\ 
    &N(\text{bonds}) = 11 \cdot N_l\\
    &N(\text{angles}) = 8 \cdot N_l\, .
\end{align}

The box dimensions derive from the area per lipid, the number density, and the total bead count determined in \cref{eq:num_beads}

\begin{align}
    V &= \frac{N(\text{beads})}{\rho}\\
    L_x = L_y &= \sqrt{N_l/2 \cdot A_l}\\
    L_z &= \frac{V}{L_x L_y}\, .
\end{align}

Assigning a mass of unity to each bead in DPD units, $\hat{m} = 1$, completes the necessary header information.

The bonds and angles section incorporate the connectivity data from \cref{tab:bond_angle_connections} for every POPC molecule. Similarly, \cref{tab:bond_angle_params} provides the equilibrium parameters. 

The remaining atoms section establishes the starting position of each bead. As these coordinates depend on the target configuration, the following sections describe the specific initialization procedures.

\section{Optimization of Input Parameters}\label{sec:input_opt}

Optimization of the remaining input parameters must precede the investigation of the self-assembly process. While a large grid search testing every combination of area per lipid $A_l$ and water-to-lipid ratio $R_{w/l}$ would identify the optimal parameter pairing at the global maximum, this approach would be computationally expensive, as it would simulate poentially irrelevant combinations. To bypass this procedure, this work employs an 
initial guess for the water to lipid ratio $R_{w/l}^{\text{init}} \approx 22$. 

Maintaining the initial ratio alongside the fixed number density, 
numerous simulations of a pre-assembled bilayer configuration consisting of 128 lipids are performed, while
varying the area per lipid.

The configuration of the atoms section, as detailed in \cref{sec:sys_setup}, generates the pre-assembled bilayer. Using the \path{POPC-em.gro} file as a structural baseline, the generation script places each molecule onto one of two grids in the $xy$-planes, separated by a fixed distance. The target area per lipid determines the lateral spacing of these grid points. To orient the lipid tails inward, the script rotates one leaflet by 180° around the x-axis. Finally, placing water beads outside the leaflets completes the atoms section and provides the solvent environment. \cref{fig:128_bilayer_setup} illustrates this setup for a system of 128 lipids.

This study comprises 15 simulations performed with 128 POPC lipids and an initial water-to-lipid ratio of $R_{w/l}=22$. The simulations explore area-per-lipid values ranging from $A_l = 0.5\si{\nano\m\squared}$ to $1.2\si{\nano\m\squared}$ at temperatures of $\hat{T}=1.0$ and $0.8$. Subsequent sections investigate the significance of these temperature choices. Each run covers a duration of $3000\tau$ DPD time units with a timestep of $\Delta \hat{t} = 0.01$, following the protocol established by Wang and Hernandez \cite{Wang2024}. To quantify the bilayer quality, the analysis utilizes the 
nematic order parameter introduced in \cref{nematic}. The calculation covers the last $2000 \tau$
of the DPD trajectories to discard effects arising from the initial grid configuration. This procedure employs the normalized head-to-tail vector as the orientation axis for determining the order parameter. 

The next investigating examines the water-to-lipid ratio to validate the initial guess. While following the previous methodology, the study now varies the water-to-lipid ratio instead of the area per lipid. During these runs, the area per lipid remains fixed at the optimal value identified in the preceding simulations. Establishing this optimal parameter pairing finally allows the self-assembly process to begin.

\section{Spontaneous Self-Assembly Protocol} \label{sec:128_assembly}

To investigate self-assembly, the simulation setup disperses the lipid and water molecules are randomly throughout the 
box. The script adjusts the atom section to generate a disordered configuration, a lipid soup, instead of the pre-assembled bilayer formation described previously.
The initialization assigns a random center of mass to each water and lipid molecule. To ensure random alignment, the procedure adds individual rotations around every axis to each lipid. Applying the periodic box condition retains every molecule within the designated box. 

This procedure highlights a significant advantage of DPD. While standard MD simulations require great care to prevent bead overlaps, which would result in high-energy starting configurations, the soft nature of the DPD minimizes the penalty for overlapping particles. Consequently, the simulation remains stable even with this simple setup scheme. \cref{fig:lipid_soup_methods} illustrates the corresponding system before self-assembly.

Equilibrating the system is achieved by running it at an elevated temperature of $\hat{T} = 2$
where high thermal energy prevents premature self-assembly. To minimize large temperature fluctuations
during the initial phase, every atom is assigned a random velocity drawn from a Gaussian distribution, 
consistent with the starting temperature. Gradually lowering the temperature to $\hat{T} = 1$, induces bilayer formation by modifying the temperature argument of the DPD force.
However, simply updating the this value causes the DPD thermostat to adjust the random force parameter $\hat{\sigma} = \sqrt{2\hat{\gamma}\hat{T}}$ instantaneously, which prevents a controlled temperature ramp.
To emulate a continuous cooling process, the simulation utilizes a loop that incrementally updates the target DPD temperature every few steps. 
After reaching the target temperature of $\hat{T} = 1$, a longer production phase
stabilizes the resulting structure.
To evaluate how lower temperatures influence the quality of the final structure, a second set of simulations repeats this procedure using a production
temperature of $\hat{T} = 0.8$.

The analysis utilizes the nematic order parameter and the contact number from \cref{nematic} to measure both the alignment and the packing density of the bilayer.
The contact number requires selecting a cutoff distance to categorize which lipids count as neighbors. The area per lipid serves as the basis for this value, as it directly corresponds to a radius $r_c = \sqrt{A_l/\pi}$. Furthermore, a
comparison to the MARTINI model is added, by calculating the same metrics for a pre-assembled and 
equilibrated MARTINI bilayer at $T = 300\si{K}$. To test whether the bilayer reached the 
target structure  
a pre-assembled DPD bilayer at both temperatures is also compared. 
Additionally, a density profile along the
bilayer axis reveals the bead distribution.  
To emphasize phase seperation, the evaluation splits the bead types into three distinct groups 

\begin{itemize}
    \item Head group: ID 1, 2, 3, 4
    \item First tail group: ID 5, 6, 9, 10 
    \item Terminal tail group: ID 7, 8, 11, 12.
\end{itemize}

\cref{fig:popc_lammps} illustrates the different bead IDs and \cref{fig:popc_groups} the bead groups.

\section{Implementation of Larger Systems}\label{sec:larger_systems}

To evaluate the finite size effect and the ability to generalize to larger systems, a system containing double the amount of lipids at 256
is utilized. While the setup mirrors the 128-lipid assembly in \cref{sec:128_assembly}, the change in system size necessitates fine-tuning of the area per lipid and the water-to-lipid ratio. This process involves slight adjustments to both parameters to ensure optimal results.

The simulation initiates at $\hat{T} = 2$ with an 
identical velocity initialization. Ensuring proper dispersion and preventing unwanted clumping of lipid tails necessitates a longer equilibration phase. The cooling 
process then proceeds until the system reaches the target temperature of $\hat{T} = 1$.

This initial configuration yields a bilayer within a non-cubic area where 
the area per lipid remains below the optimal value.
To achieve a square-shaped bilayer, the simulation gradually deforms the box, adjusting the dimensions while maintaining a constant volume.   

The production phase follows the deformation process. Due to the increased system size, a longer total simulation time is required. The analysis of bilayer quality follows the methodology described in \cref{sec:128_assembly}, comparing the results against the same MARTINI baseline and DPD bilayers of matching system size.

\section{Simulations of membrane Fluctuations}\label{sec:method_ben_mod_fourier}

The determination of the POPC membrane bending modulus utilizes the theoretical foundation of \cref{sec:ben_mod_fourier}. This process involves measuring the power spectrum of each mode in \cref{eq:cental_ben_formula_fourier} and applying a corresponding fit to derive the bending modulus. However, several physical constraints complicate this procedure


Continuum theory breaks down when the inverse wave vector 1/k becomes comparable to the bilayer thickness w. Consequently, the investigation must focus on small wave vectors. However, for values smaller than the critical wavelength $k_c = \sqrt{\Sigma/\kappa}$ the lateral tension dominates. The simulations must therefore run at zero lateral tension to ensure the target scaling of $k^{-4}$. Maintaining this condition is difficult by manually adjusting the box dimensions is difficult \cite{Cooke_bending_mod}. To circumvent this, a Nose/Hoover barostat \cite{LammpsNPH} adjusts the box volume to maintain a specific target pressure, which is simply the average pressure given by LAMMPS for simulations without a barostat. Choosing a low damping parameter, which governs the reaction speed of the barostat, ensures the achievement of zero tension, while using a reduced timestep of $\Delta \hat{t} = 0.001\tau$. The timestep adjustment is central as for low damping parameters a large change in pressure leads to a large change in the box volume. If the time step is too large pressure spikes are more common, leading to oscillation or heavy reshaping of the box dimensions. The system must be substantially larger than the self-assembly setup to accommodate small wave vectors.

The simulation begins with a pre-assembled membrane containing 4096 lipids, following the methodology of \cref{sec:input_opt} and the work of Cook and Deserno \cite{Cooke_bending_mod}. After an equilibration phase of 100$\tau$ to eliminate grid-initialization effects, 100 samples of the system are taken. To generate a height function the mean tail position of each lipid is interpolated on a $16 \times 16$ grid. This interpolation requires careful handling, as it introduces discretization artifacts, discussed by Cook and Deserno \cite{Cooke_bending_mod}.
The calculation must account for a damping factor for the two-dimensional wave numbers $(n_x, n_y)$ and an $N \times N$ grid

\begin{align}
    \mathrm{sinc}^2\left(\frac{n_x}{N}\right)\mathrm{sinc}^2\left(\frac{n_y}{N}\right)\, .
\end{align}

Dividing out this factor resolves its $k$-dependence, which would otherwise interfere with the results. A two-dimensional Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) then processes the grid data. The absolute value squared is averaged and is ordered into bins of corresponding $k$ values. As the lateral tension is zero a simple $k^{-4}$ fit of

\begin{align}
    L^2 \langle |\tilde{h}_k|^2 \rangle L^2 \approx \frac{k_\text{B}T}{\kappa} k^{-4}
\end{align}

reveals the bending modulus of the POPC membrane. \cref{fig:spectrum_pipeline_final_aligned} visualizes the pipeline of the bending modulus measurement.

\section{Determined input parameters}

Measuring the nematic order parameter reveals the bilayer stability for various areas per lipid at $\hat{T}=1.0$ and $\hat{T}=0.8$, as shown in \cref{fig:apl_T_1} and \cref{fig:apl_T_08}. 
Both curves exhibit a comparable shape, characterized by reduced alignment at extreme values  
and a maximum in the intermediate range.
\cref{fig:apl_mol_05}, \cref{fig:apl_mol_09}, and \cref{fig:apl_mol_12} display snapshots representing these three regions. 
A lower area per lipid restricts the available space for the lipids, inducing structural instabilities and preventing distinct phase separation between heads and tails. Conversely, excessive space at elevated areas per lipid triggers the formation of hole defects within the bilayer. The 
membrane at an area per lipid of $0.9\si{\nano\m\squared}$ shows distinct phase separation 
between heads and tails and reaches a degree of alignment characteristic of a fluid lipid bilayer.

Reduced thermal excitation allows the curve at $\hat{T}=0.8$ to attain significantly higher values around the maximum. While the maxima of both curves do not coincide perfectly, both occur near $0.85\si{\nano\m\squared}$. The 128-lipid self-assembly system employs this value, whereas the larger 256-lipid system utilizes an elevated value of $0.87\si{\nano\m\squared}$.

A comparison between the selected area per lipid and the typical values for a POPC MARTINI bilayer $(0.65\si{\nano\m\squared})$ and an all-atom bilayer $(0.684\si{\nano\m\squared})$ \cite{all_atom_popc} reveal significant differences. Examining the respective potentials of both methods clarifies this distinction. The effective bead size, defined as the distance at which the repulsive force ends, strongly influences the area per lipid. In the MARTINI model, the Lennard-Jones potential minimum dictates this size, varying with the specific bead types. For a POPC and water system composed solely of regular beads, the $\sigma$ parameter governing the effective size equals $0.47\si{\nano\m}$. Calculating the Lennard-Jones potential minimum yields $1.12\sigma\approx0.53\si{\nano\m}$. Since the DPD potential lacks a minimum, a force analysis identifies the effective bead size as the cutoff value of $0.711\si{\nano\m}$. This discrepancy in the underlying forces and potentials drives the observed difference in area per lipid, as the effective size of MARTINI beads is smaller than the DPD beads. This represents a fundamental distinction between this implementation of CG DPD and MARTINI.

As described in \cref{sec:input_opt}, validating the water-to-lipid ratio remains essential, as the previous simulation runs utilized a constant value of 22. \cref{fig:wpl_T_1} and \cref{fig:wpl_T_08} present the results for bilayer stability at $\hat{T}=1.0$ and $\hat{T}=0.8$.

The curves again display a similar shape: the order parameter increases as the water-to-lipid ratio rises before leveling off at higher ratios. The large overlap between the error bars highlights this trend.

Pre-assembled DPD and MARTINI bilayers provide a quantitative comparison.

The order gradually rises during the cooling phase and plateaus in the production phases. The production phase exhibits fluctuations mainly attributable to thermal noise.

The contact number in \cref{fig:128_T_1_con} follows a similar trend, although an initial rise indicates that lipids form clumps even at elevated temperatures despite the lack of alignment. The cooling process leads to a rising contact number, while the production phase reaches a plateau where thermal fluctuations drive further variance.

The comparison confirms a successful self-assembly, as both the nematic order parameter and the contact number reach the values of the pre-assembled DPD bilayer. However, the MARTINI bilayer exhibits significant deviations, particularly regarding the system's order. The MARTINI bilayer attains a mean value of approximately 0.8, whereas the DPD bilayer reaches maximum values of only 0.6. Consequently, the MARTINI model allows for significantly higher alignment than the DPD-based approach. Furthermore, the DPD contact number remains lower than the MARTINI value, suggesting a reduced packing density in comparison.

\section{Bending modulus by power spectrum}

Applying the method from \cref{sec:method_ben_mod_fourier} to DPD and MARTINI bilayers yields two distinct power spectra. \cref{fig:dpd_slope} and \cref{fig:martini_slope} exhibit the characteristic scaling of the power spectrum for both systems. Fitting the data according to \cref{eq:cental_ben_formula_fourier} determines the bending modulus for each membrane.

All-atom simulations of tension-free POPC bilayers also utilizing the height spectra \cite{all_atom_bending} revealed a value of $(23.3 \pm 1.4)k_\text{B}T$. The softer DPD force permits significantly greater lipid mobility within the membrane, as neighboring lipids do not strongly restrict thermal fluctuations. \chapter{Conclusion}

This paper implements the DPD force field, introduced by Groot \cite{DPD_groot_warren}, by utilizing the MARTINI method as a baseline \cite{Wang2024}. Testing the resulting coarse-graining method on a POPC membrane involved simulating self-assembly for multiple system sizes and temperatures, which determined key properties including the area per lipid, the membrane thickness, and the bending modulus. The study computed the latter using a power spectrum analysis \cite{Cooke_bending_mod}.

The area per lipid and thickness showed clear deviations from the MARTINI and all-atom values. The area per lipid increased because the effective size of the DPD beads exceeded that of MARTINI. The softness of the DPD interaction reduced the bilayer thickness. The difficulty of self-assembly depended strongly on the system size; while smaller membranes assembled without issues, simulations containing larger lipid quantities formed alternative structures and required further intervention to ensure a membrane. Generally, the self-assembly proved successful and computationally efficient, yielding a structure that strongly resembles the MARTINI result. The DPD bending modulus also decreased significantly, which stems from a membrane that allows much larger height fluctuations. Notably, guaranteeing a tension-free membrane in DPD remains challenging, as the pressure depends strongly on the repulsion parameters and thus proves difficult to interpret in SI units.

Future work could address these deviations from MARTINI and all-atom methods by adjusting the force field. One key aspect involves the cutoff parameter of the DPD force, as it represents the main contributor to the distinct area per lipid. Reducing the cutoff parameter to match the effective size of MARTINI beads could improve comparability between both methods. Crucially, other discrepancies stem from the fundamental formalism of DPD. The softness induces significant molecular mobility, as overlaps entail only minimal energy penalties. Thus, resolving the deviations in thickness and bending modulus in this implementation might prove difficult.
